Green cities, seen through water

The city that must keep its feet wet

Amsterdam lies largely below sea level behind dikes and pumps. You would expect its water managers to worry mainly about floods. They do. Yet thousands of the city's buildings stand on wooden piles that last for centuries only while they stay under water. Too much water floods basements. Too little lets the piles rot.

This summer (2026) made the paradox vivid: the Netherlands, the country famous for keeping water out, declared a threatened water shortage on 1 July and an actual water shortage on 16 July, as a drought approached the record set in 1976. A green city has to handle floods and droughts in the same streets, often in the same year.

Stop 1 of 7

A country that pumps itself dry to stay afloat

About a thousand years ago, people began digging ditches to drain the peat bogs of Holland so they could farm. Drained peat does two things: it compacts, and it rots as air reaches it. The land sank. Farmers dug deeper ditches, and the land sank again. Over a millennium, this loop lowered the Dutch coastal plain by about 1.9 m on average.

Windmills, then steam and electric pumping stations, kept the new low land (a polder) dry by lifting water, step by step, into canals and rivers. The deepest polder in Amsterdam's own water district lies 6.7 m below the national reference level, NAP, which sits close to mean sea level.

Here is the catch. Water always flows from high to low pressure, so the lower you pump a polder, the harder the surrounding groundwater pushes up from below. The Dutch call this upwelling kwel (seepage). In some deep polders it drags up old, brackish seawater left behind when the sea covered the land thousands of years ago.

Stop 2 of 7

Drinking the Rhine, filtered through a beach

In the mid-1800s Amsterdam drank from canals that doubled as open sewers, and cholera followed. On 12 December 1853, clean water from the coastal dunes first flowed to a tap at the city's Haarlemmerpoort. Rain that soaks into dune sand forms a freshwater lens: a body of fresh groundwater floating on the denser seawater beneath.

Success bred trouble. As the city grew, pumping thinned the lens. Why so fast? Because the lens obeys a lever with a 40-to-1 ratio. Two Dutch engineers, Drabbe and Badon Ghijben (1889), and the German Herzberg (1901) worked it out while planning wells for Amsterdam and the German coast: for every metre the water table stands above sea level, fresh water extends about 40 m below it.

Since 1957 Amsterdam has refilled the dunes with pre-treated river water taken from the Lek canal near Nieuwegein, which is fed by the Lek, a branch of the Rhine. The water spends two to three months in the sand before it is pumped out and treated further at Leiduin. The dunes now supply about two-thirds of Amsterdam's drinking water, roughly 70 million m³ a year.

Why the dunes? They filter out bacteria, viruses and organic contaminants, even out swings in water quality and temperature, and store a buffer for days when the river intake has to close. They also defend the coast, shelter nature, and attract visitors: four jobs that do not always agree.

Georgia connection

Same lever, Savannah edition

Coastal Georgia draws much of its water from the Upper Floridan aquifer. Heavy pumping around Savannah lowered the aquifer's freshwater pressure so far that groundwater flow reversed, and seawater began entering the aquifer beneath Port Royal Sound. Hilton Head Island's utility has lost at least six of its twelve Upper Floridan wells since 2000 and has run a reverse-osmosis plant since 2009. Georgia responded with a coastal "Red Zone" (Chatham and southern Effingham counties), where most permit limits were cut by 22% for 2025. The geometry differs from a dune lens (a confined aquifer, not a floating lens), but the rule is the same: lower the freshwater pressure and salt water moves in.

Amsterdam's demand is expected to grow from about 95 million m³ a year (2023) to roughly 122 million by 2050, so the city is testing new sources. Since 2024 a pilot plant has turned brackish seepage from the deep Horstermeer polder into drinking water by reverse osmosis. That solves two problems at once, since the salty seepage also harms nature in the polder. But salt never disappears; it only moves.

Stop 3 of 7

A city on a buried forest

Amsterdam's soft peat and clay cannot carry a brick house, so builders drove wooden piles down to a firm sand layer. The Royal Palace on Dam Square, built from 1648, stands on 13,659 of them. Under water, the wood lasts for centuries because wood-rotting fungi need three things: moisture, food and oxygen. Groundwater keeps the oxygen out.

So the city manages groundwater within a narrow window. If groundwater rises too high, crawl spaces and cellars flood and walls grow mould. If it falls below the pile tops, air reaches the wood, fungi go to work, and houses crack and tilt. Since 2008, Dutch municipalities have had a legal duty of care for groundwater in public space (property owners remain responsible for their own plots). Amsterdam reads groundwater by hand at roughly 2,500 locations six times a year, supported by hundreds of automatic loggers. Its records begin in 1870.

Drought threatens more than piles. On 26 August 2003, after a hot, dry summer, a dried-out peat levee at Wilnis, south of Amsterdam, slid sideways and flooded a neighbourhood; 1,500 to 2,000 residents were evacuated. In July 2026 the national water agency named that failure again as the kind of risk a drought brings.

Stop 4 of 7

When rain meets pavement

On a forested or grassy surface, most rain soaks in or returns to the air. Pave the ground and the budget flips: in the U.S. EPA's classic comparison, runoff rises from about 10% of rainfall under natural cover to about 55% where 75–100% of the ground is sealed, while deep infiltration drops from 25% to 5%.

Runoff goes into pipes. Dutch sewers have traditionally been designed around a test storm of about 20 mm in one hour ("bui 08"), which falls roughly once every two years. Many older districts in Amsterdam, like downtown Atlanta, have combined sewers that carry rainwater and wastewater in one pipe. A typical Dutch combined system can store only about 7 mm of rain and pumps about 0.7 mm per hour to the treatment plant. Beyond that, the mixture spills into canals or creeks: a combined sewer overflow.

Amsterdam's last canal houses in the city centre were connected to the sewer only in 1987; the last houseboats followed in 2017.

Georgia connection

Atlanta's combined sewers

About 10% of Atlanta's sewer collection system, in a roughly 13-square-mile area around downtown, is combined. Overflows into the Chattahoochee's tributaries led to a federal consent decree in 1998, after a lawsuit by the EPA, Georgia EPD, the Upper Chattahoochee Riverkeeper and a downstream resident.

Stop 5 of 7

Where do you put 60 millimetres in an hour?

On 2 July 2011 a cloudburst hit Copenhagen. Some places received more than 50 mm in 30 minutes; the Botanical Garden logged 135 mm for the day. Insurers received about 90,000 damage claims worth roughly 5 to 6 billion Danish kroner. Amsterdam took note and launched its Rainproof programme in 2014. Six months later, on 28 July 2014, a morning downpour flooded Amsterdam's streets and tunnels.

Pipes cannot solve this alone: a sewer big enough for a once-a-century storm would sit nearly empty for decades. So Amsterdam plans for about 60 mm in one hour (roughly a 1-in-100-year hour in the Netherlands) by making the whole city hold water like a sponge. Since 2021, new buildings and major renovations must store 60 litres per square metre of footprint and release it slowly, at no more than 1 litre per square metre per hour.

The KNMI weather service finds that it does not rain more often in the Netherlands, but it rains harder: days with more than 20 mm rose from 3.7 a year (1961–1990) to 4.6 a year (1991–2020).

During the 1-hour storm each square metre of garden can hold its depth d plus what soaks away (f × 1 h), minus the rain that falls on it directly. Required area W = A·P / (d + f·1 h − P). Emptying time = d / f. Amsterdam's green strips are designed to empty within 24 hours.

Georgia connection

Atlanta's first inch

Since 2013, Atlanta's post-development stormwater ordinance has required nearly all new development and redevelopment to manage the first inch (25.4 mm) of rain on site with green infrastructure. The city also builds parks that flood on purpose: Historic Fourth Ward Park on the BeltLine (opened 2011) replaced a planned underground storage tunnel with a stormwater pond, and the 16-acre Rodney Cook Sr. Park in Vine City (2021), built where homes flooded in 2002, can hold up to about 10 million gallons of stormwater.

Stop 6 of 7

Trees: air conditioners that drink groundwater

Brick, asphalt and roofs soak up sunlight by day and release it at night, so cities stay warmer than their surroundings. In Dutch cities this urban heat island averages less than 3 °C over a summer, but the gap widens on hot evenings, and nights matter most for health.

Trees cool a street in two ways: shade, and transpiration. Every litre a tree moves from soil to air absorbs about 2.45 megajoules as it evaporates. In May 2019, sap-flow sensors on six Amsterdam elms recorded midday peaks of roughly 2 to 8 litres per hour for five trees and nearly 40 litres per hour for the sixth (MSc thesis, Neart 2020).

Now join the stops. Water a tree transpires in a dry summer comes from the same groundwater that keeps the pile tops wet. Green measures interact, so they must be designed together: trees beside infiltrating rain gardens, not trees on a sealed street.

38 L/h

Cooling power = transpiration (kg/s) × 2.45 MJ/kg. A 3-ton central air conditioner removes about 10.5 kW of heat. This is the energy the evaporation absorbs; how much cooler the street feels also depends on shade, wind and humidity.

Stop 7 of 7

Same physics, different numbers: Atlanta

Atlanta straddles the Eastern Continental Divide, high in its river basins, on granite and other crystalline rock that holds little usable groundwater. More than 99% of metro Atlanta's water comes from rivers, streams and reservoirs, about 71% from Lake Lanier and the Chattahoochee. Amsterdam's lesson is not a list of gadgets. It is that a green city is a water-storage problem in disguise. Every roof, street and tree decides where a raindrop goes, how long it stays, and whose basement, pile or river it reaches. Three questions we cannot yet answer well, for Amsterdam or for Atlanta:

  1. If storms grow more intense while droughts grow longer, should a city store water on the surface, push it into the ground, or both, and who decides for each street?
  2. Green infrastructure is judged by how much water it holds. How would you measure what it does to groundwater, stream baseflow and water quality over ten years, rather than during one storm?
  3. Amsterdam's integrated water-cycle utility, Waternet, was split into two organisations on 1 January 2026, for governance reasons. Is managing the whole water cycle under one roof the ideal, or is coordination between separate agencies the more robust design?
