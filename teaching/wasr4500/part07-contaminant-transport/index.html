Contaminant Transport

Spill a drum of solvent on the ground above an aquifer and come back in a year. The ground water beneath it has moved a few tens of meters; the solvent has not kept up. Some of it is still clinging to the first grains it touched, some has escaped back up through the soil as vapor, some has been eaten by bacteria, and what is left in the water has smeared into a plume far longer than the distance the water itself traveled, at a concentration far below what you spilled. Seven processes did that. The water tells one story about where it is going; the contaminant tells seven at once. The point of this Part is that we can write every one of those stories down, one control volume at a time, until they collapse into a single equation that predicts where the plume will be next year. Learning objectives — what you should be able to do

  2. Derive the advective flux vx n C from concentration, discharge, and cross-sectional area, and explain why the contaminant travels at the average linear velocity rather than the Darcy flux.
  3. Write the net outflow of mass from a control volume for advection and for dispersion, and interpret its sign as depletion or accretion.
  4. Apply Fick’s law in water and in a porous medium, and estimate an effective diffusion coefficient from porosity and tortuosity with the Freeze & Cherry, Helfferich, Bear, Greenkorn & Kessler, and Berner models.

  7. Combine mechanical dispersion and molecular diffusion into the hydrodynamic dispersion coefficient and judge, with numbers, when diffusion matters.

Chapter 1 · The mechanisms of contaminant transport

Seven Fates of a Molecule

Here is the puzzle of this chapter. Ground water in an aquifer moves at one speed — the average linear velocity — and if contaminants simply rode along, a plume would be a slug of the original concentration sliding downgradient at that speed. Real plumes arrive early and late, thinner and wider, and lighter than what was spilled. Something is spreading the slug, something is holding part of it back, and something is destroying it. Those somethings have names, and the first job is to learn which does what.

1 · The cast

What can happen to a dissolved contaminant

The text opens with a one-sentence organizing principle: the major physical, chemical, and biological processes either limit contaminant migration, dilute contaminant concentrations, or remove contaminant mass from the water system. Keep those three verbs — limit, dilute, remove — in front of you; they are the test every mechanism below has to pass. The lecture groups the same cast by family: physical processes (advection, mechanical dispersion, volatilization), chemical processes (molecular diffusion, sorption), and a biological one (biodegradation). Notice that one entry on the list is not a mechanism of its own at all: hydrodynamic dispersion is the name we give to mechanical dispersion and molecular diffusion taken together, because in the field we cannot tell them apart. Click each card to read the text’s definition and what the process does to a plume.

2 · Watch them act

The mechanism switchboard

Definitions are easy to nod at and hard to hold. The switchboard below releases a spill of 600 particles just below the water table and lets you turn the mechanisms on one at a time. Advection carries the whole cloud at v. Mechanical dispersion smears it along the flow (and a little across it), by an amount that grows with v — switch the flow off and mechanical dispersion stops too, because it is the flow, taking many roads at once. Molecular diffusion spreads the cloud whether the water moves or not, but so slowly that you must run the clock for years to see it. Sorption parks a fraction of the particles on the grains, so the cloud as a whole drifts at v/R; biodegradation deletes particles at a rate set by a half-life; volatilization removes those that wander up into the capillary fringe. Two markers keep score: where the center of the cloud would be with advection alone (v t) and where it actually is.

What the markers teach. Dispersion and diffusion move the center of the cloud not at all — they dilute without delaying. Sorption delays without diluting the total mass (count the parked particles and they are all still there). Biodegradation and volatilization are the only switches that make particles disappear — the only two that remove mass from the water system. That is the text’s opening sentence, acted out.
3 · The one that stays behind

Sorption and retardation

Adsorption (sorption) is the process by which a contaminant moves, reversibly or irreversibly, from the aqueous phase to the solid phase. It can dominate the fate of a chemical released to any phase in contact with a solid, and how much of it happens depends on the pairing of sorbents (the surfaces a chemical can stick to) and sorbates (the chemicals that stick), and on the chemistry of the water — above all its pH. Sorbents carry charged surface sites, and both the sign and the magnitude of that charge change with pH. The most important sorbents in natural waters are organic matter and the oxides and hydroxides of iron and manganese, which form coatings on ordinary mineral grains; the oxides and hydroxides of aluminum matter too. Slide the pH below and watch an oxide coating change its mind about what it attracts.

Three consequences follow, and the text states each of them as a warning. First, sorption onto the aquifer matrix reduces mobile-phase concentrations but does not remove the contaminant from the water system: a sorbed contaminant is in storage, not gone, and a change in pH, redox state, or flow can bring it back — which is why a plume that has “disappeared” can reappear years later. Second, contaminants sorbed onto mobile colloids are mobile: a molecule riding a suspended particle moves with the water however strongly it is bound. Third, reversible sorption makes a sorbing species travel at a velocity smaller than the average linear ground-water velocity — the sorbate is retarded. A convenient way to say how much is the retardation factor R, the ratio of the water’s velocity to the contaminant’s: a species with R = 3 moves one meter for every three the water moves, so a plume that would arrive at a well in a year under advection alone takes three. Retardation is the text’s way of saying that contaminants may move more slowly than advection predicts because of chemical and physical reactions with the solid particles of the subsurface.

4 · Sort the seven

What does each one do to the plume?

Tag each process with its effect. Carries — moves the plume at the water’s speed. Dilutes — spreads it out so concentrations fall while the mass stays. Retards — holds part of it back on the solids without removing it from the system. Removes — takes mass out of the water, to another phase or to other chemicals. Use the text’s own words as the key.

Chapter 2 · Advection and the control volume

Carried by the Flow

Advection is the simplest of the seven: the contaminant goes where the water goes, at the water’s speed. It is also the one most often computed wrong by a factor of three, because the number that comes out of Darcy’s law is not the speed of the water. This chapter builds the advective flux from the definition of concentration in six short steps, and then asks the question every transport equation is built on: what does a small box of aquifer see when more mass leaves than arrives?

1 · Six steps

From concentration to flux

Advection considers a contaminant to be moving at the average linear velocity, v, of the water. Everything else in the chapter is bookkeeping with units, and it is worth doing slowly once. The concentration of a contaminant in the water phase, C, is the mass of contaminant per volume of water (mg/L or μg/L); the discharge Q is the volume of water per unit time; so their product is a mass per unit time — the rate at which contaminant passes a cross section. Divide by the area of that cross section and you have a flux, mass per unit time per unit area. One more substitution turns the flux into something a hydrogeologist can measure in the field: Darcy’s Q/A is the specific discharge qx, and because the water moves only through the pores, qx = n vx.

C = mass of contaminant / volume of water Eq A
Q = volume of water / unit time  ⇒  C·Q = mass of contaminant / unit time Eqs B and C
flux = mass / (unit time · cross-sectional area) = C·Q/A    Q/A = qx = n vx Eqs D, E and F
advective fluxx = vx n C Eq G — the mass of contaminant per unit time per unit cross-sectional area carried by advection in the x-direction

The porosity in Eq G is the whole point. A tracer in an aquifer with n = 0.3 travels at v = q/n, more than three times the Darcy flux, because the same discharge has to squeeze through a third of the cross section. Forget the n and your plume arrives three years late. Put the six steps in order, then build a flux with the sliders and watch the units cancel.

2 · A box in the aquifer

Inflow, outflow, and the sign that tells you which is winning

Now draw a small box in the aquifer with sides dx, dy, dz. The mass per unit time entering through its upstream face by advection is the flux of Eq G times the area of that face:

(mass/unit time) inflow in the x-direction = vx n C dy dz Eq H

What leaves through the downstream face need not be the same, because vx C can change over the length dx. The text’s way of saying this is worth memorizing because it recurs in every derivation of the course: the change in mass per unit time across the box is the rate of change of mass per unit time in the direction of flow, multiplied by the distance in that direction. If the mass rate changes by −2 μg per day per foot of travel and the box is 10 ft long, the mass rate changes by −20 μg per day across it. In calculus, take the derivative of Eq H with respect to x and multiply by dx:

change in (mass/unit time) in the x-direction = n [∂(vx C)/∂x] dx dy dz Eq I — outflow minus inflow: the net outflow of contaminant mass per unit time from the box by advection

Eq I is outflow minus inflow, and its sign carries the physics. When it is positive, more leaves than arrives and the box has suffered a depletion of contaminant mass. When it is negative, less leaves than arrives and the box has experienced an accretion. Set the gradient below, read the inflow and outflow on the two faces, then work the text’s own example.

Chapter 3 · Molecular diffusion, mechanical dispersion, and hydrodynamic dispersion

Spreading Out

Put a drop of ink in still water and it spreads — slowly, by diffusion, at a rate you can look up in a table. Put the same drop in water flowing through sand and it spreads thousands of times faster, not because the molecules move faster but because the water takes a thousand different roads. Separating those two kinds of spreading is impossible in a real aquifer, which is why hydrologists invented a third quantity that adds them together. This chapter builds all three and ends with an example in which diffusion turns out to be four percent of the story.

1 · Darcy’s twin

Molecular diffusion and Fick’s law

Even with no flow at all, contaminants drift from where they are concentrated to where they are not. The rate depends on the concentration gradient, and the law is Fick’s law — which, the text points out, is analogous to Darcy’s law: a flux proportional to a gradient, with a minus sign because things run downhill.

Fx = −Dw,x ∂C/∂x Eq K — Fx: flux by molecular diffusion in the x-direction [mass/(t·L2)]; Dw,x: diffusion coefficient in water [L2/t]; ∂C/∂x: concentration gradient [mass/L4]

Diffusion coefficients in water are small numbers with a narrow range: most ions and common organic contaminants sit between about 5 and 30 × 10−6 cm2/s at 25 °C, and many common organic contaminants have diffusion coefficients on the order of 10 to 20 × 10−6. The one spectacular exception is the hydrogen ion, which does not push through the water so much as hop from one water molecule to the next. Pick a species on the ladder, set a gradient, and see what Fick’s law delivers — and how far diffusion alone can carry it in a day, a year, a century.

2 · Two corrections

The maze: diffusion in a porous medium

Eq K describes diffusion in a beaker. An aquifer needs two corrections. First, only the pores carry water, so to express the flux per unit area of porous medium we multiply by the porosity:

Fx = −n Dw,x ∂C/∂x Eq L — x-direction molecular diffusion in the porous medium

Second, the apparent or effective diffusion coefficient, D*, of a contaminant in a porous medium is considerably smaller than in water, because the paths are convoluted: a molecule cannot diffuse straight through a sand grain, so it goes around. Several semi-quantitative models relate D* to Dw. Some use a simple fraction ω (Freeze & Cherry); some bound it with the porosity (Helfferich); the rest use the tortuosity τ, the ratio of the length of the flow path to the length of the sample, which Bear puts between 1.25 and 1.79:

Freeze & Cherry (1979): D* = ω Dw, 0.01 < ω < 0.5   Helfferich (1966): Dw [n/(2 − n)]2 < D* < Dw n/2τ = (length of flow path)/(length of sample); Bear (1972): 1.25 < τ < 1.79
Bear (1972): D* = Dw/τ   Greenkorn & Kessler (1972): D* = n Dw/τ   Berner (1971): D* = n Dw/τ2
diffusive fluxx = −n D*x ∂C/∂x Eq M — the mass of contaminant per unit time per unit cross-sectional area moved by diffusion in the x-direction

Set the porosity and the tortuosity and compare the five models side by side. They do not agree, and the spread between them is the honest answer to how well D* is known: within a factor of ten, unless you measure it.

The control-volume argument of Chapter 2 now repeats word for word with the diffusive flux in place of the advective one. The inflow through the upstream face is Eq M times dy dz (Eq N); the change across the box is its x-derivative times dx (Eq O); positive means depletion, negative means accretion; and adding the three directions gives the net outflow by diffusion:

net outflow from the CV = −n [ ∂(D*x ∂C/∂x)/∂x + ∂(D*y ∂C/∂y)/∂y + ∂(D*z ∂C/∂z)/∂z ] dx dy dz Eq P
3 · A thousand roads

Mechanical dispersion

Water particles that leave the same point at the same time do not arrive downgradient together. Some thread wide pores and run ahead; some take the long way around a grain; some wander into a dead-end pore and wait. Mechanical dispersion is the resulting scatter of local velocities about the mean linear velocity. Unlike molecular diffusion, which is a chemical process, mechanical dispersion is a physical one, set by the particular porous medium — and the medium misbehaves at every scale. Domenico & Schwartz list the culprits by size: microscopic heterogeneities (pore-size distribution, pore geometry, dead-end pores), macroscopic ones (nonuniform stratification, stratification continuities and discontinuities, directional permeability trends), and megascopic ones (faults, dipping formations, extremely permeable rock fractures). Run the pore race and switch the microscopic causes on one at a time; the histogram of arrival times is the spreading you would measure as a breakthrough curve.

Dispersion has a direction. Along the mean flow it is longitudinal dispersion, quantified by the longitudinal mechanical dispersion coefficient D′L; normal to the flow but parallel to the bedding it is lateral or transverse dispersion, D′T; normal to the flow and perpendicular to a horizontal bedding plane it is vertical dispersion, D′V. All three have units of L2/t, like a diffusion coefficient, and field and column studies agree on their simplest description: each is directly proportional to the average linear velocity, with a coefficient — a length — that belongs to the medium:

D′L = αL v   D′T = αT v   D′V = αV v Eqs Q — αL, αT, αV: the longitudinal, transverse, and vertical dispersivities [L]; in the field αL is of the order of meters, αT of tenths of a meter, αV of hundredths
4 · The ruler changes the answer

Dispersivity and the scale effect

Here is the awkward fact that makes dispersivity unlike any material property you have met: it depends on how big an experiment you do. Laboratory columns give αL of the order of 0.01 to 1 cm; short-distance field experiments, 0.1 to 2 m; long-distance field experiments, 10 m or more. The reason is the ladder of heterogeneities above: a column sees only pore-scale variation, a 50-m tracer test crosses layers, a kilometer crosses facies and faults, and each new kind of heterogeneity adds its own spreading. Two empirical relations put a number on the scale effect, with L the non-tortuous flow-path length — the length of porous medium the experiment spans — and both L and αL in meters:

Neuman (1990): αL = 0.0175 L1.46, L < 3500 m    Xu & Eckstein (1995): αL = 0.83 (log L)2.414

Work the text’s example — field experiments spanning about 50 m — then explore the two curves. Notice where they cross, what Xu & Eckstein’s logarithm does at L = 1 m, and how far apart two respectable published relations can be.

5 · Adding the two

Hydrodynamic dispersion

In a real aquifer the spreading by molecular diffusion and the spreading by mechanical dispersion cannot be told apart, so we stop trying and define the hydrodynamic dispersion coefficient, D, to account for both:

D = D′ + D*    for one-dimensional flow: D = αL vx + D*
hydrodynamic dispersive fluxx = −n (D′x + D*x) ∂C/∂x Eq R

In general mechanical dispersion is far more significant than molecular diffusion, so the hydrodynamic coefficient is essentially the mechanical one. The text tests that claim with a case built to favor diffusion: a slow aquifer (K = 2 ft/day, gradient 0.0005, n = 0.3), benzene with Dw of 1 to 2 × 10−5 cm2/s, the most generous ω = 0.5, and an ordinary field dispersivity of 2 m. Work it step by step, then use the explorer to find the velocity at which diffusion would finally pull even.

When does diffusion win? Mechanical dispersion scales with v; diffusion does not. The two are equal when v = D*/αL — for D* = 10−5 cm2/s and αL = 2 m that is 5 × 10−8 cm/s, about 1.6 cm per year. Only in nearly stagnant water — clay aquitards, the matrix of a fractured rock, deep-sea sediments — does molecular diffusion take over. Everywhere ground water actually flows, the thousand roads win.

Chapter 4 · Developing the advection–dispersion equation

The Equation

Three budgets for one box — what dispersion sends out, what advection sends out, and what the box keeps — and one bookkeeping rule: what the box keeps is the opposite of what it sends out. Say that sentence in calculus and you have the equation that every contaminant-transport model solves, from a spreadsheet to the codes used at Superfund sites. This chapter assembles it, dissects it, and then runs it, because an equation you have watched move is one you will not forget.

1 · Three budgets

Assemble the equation

Chapter 3 ended with the net outflow by diffusion, Eq P. Replace the diffusion coefficients in it with the hydrodynamic dispersion coefficients D = D′ + D* and it becomes the net outflow due to dispersion (Eq A below). Chapter 2 ended with the net outflow by advection (Eq J there, Eq B here). Both are in mass per unit time, and either can be positive (the box is losing mass) or negative (gaining). The third ingredient is the box’s own contents: the chemical mass in the control volume is the concentration times the water volume, n dx dy dz, so its rate of change is Eq C:

net outflow due to dispersion = −n [ ∂(Dx ∂C/∂x)/∂x + ∂(Dy ∂C/∂y)/∂y + ∂(Dz ∂C/∂z)/∂z ] dx dy dz Eq A
net outflow due to advection = n [ ∂(vx C)/∂x + ∂(vy C)/∂y + ∂(vz C)/∂z ] dx dy dz Eq B
mass in CV = n C dx dy dz  ⇒  ∂m/∂t = n (∂C/∂t) dx dy dz Eq C

For ground water in which only advection and hydrodynamic dispersion matter, the sum of the two net outflows must equal the loss (or gain) of mass inside the box. The sign is the whole derivation: the mass inside rises when the net outflow is negative, so Eq C is opposite in sign but equal in magnitude to the sum of Eq A and Eq B. Put the three pieces in their places, with the right signs, and let the box’s volume and porosity cancel.

The 3-D advection–dispersion equation provides the basis for the quantification of contaminant transport via analytical or numerical modeling — that is the text’s last sentence, and the rest of this chapter is what it means.

3 · Running

The equation in motion

For one-dimensional flow with uniform v and D, a source held at C0 from t = 0, and a clean aquifer ahead of it, Ogata and Banks (1961) solved the equation exactly, and Bear (1979) extended the solution to a contaminant that decays. 4 · Backwards

The inverse problem — a warm-up for PBA 4

Everything so far ran the equation forward: parameters in, curve out. The field hands you the opposite. A column experiment or a tracer test gives you the breakthrough curve, and the question is what v, D, R, and λ produced it — the inverse problem that STANMOD solves by least squares in PBA 4. Try it by hand first. The points below are “measured” concentrations at the outlet of a 30-cm column (with a little noise); slide the parameters until the curve runs through them, and watch the misfit. Then reveal the truth and look for the lesson every inverse modeler learns: some parameters leave fingerprints on different parts of the curve, and some can trade places with each other.

Fingerprints. The arrival time of the half-concentration pins down v/R — but not v and R separately, which is why a conservative tracer (R = 1) is run first. The steepness of the rise is D, and a decaying solute is recognized by a plateau that never reaches C0: the first thing to check in a data set before fitting anything is whether the curve levels off at 1.
