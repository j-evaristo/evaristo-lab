Runoff Generation and Streamflow

A stream is a watershed telling you what it remembers. Drop a storm on a hillside and most of the water vanishes — into the soil, into plants, into aquifers — yet a few hours later the creek at the bottom rises, often with water that fell weeks ago. The hydrograph at a gauging station is the record of that response, and almost everything hydrologists are paid to do — forecast floods, size culverts, protect water quality, keep rivers alive through droughts — depends on reading it correctly. This companion follows the water from raindrop to record: the watershed and its hydrograph, the surprising paths water takes to the channel, what a gauge can tell you about a river’s character, and the methods engineers use to predict the flood that has not happened yet. Chapter 1 · The watershed, the stream network, and the storm hydrograph

From Raindrop to Record

Here is the puzzle of this chapter. A storm drops 38 millimeters on a Vermont hillside and the stream below rises within hours — yet when the water is added up, less than a tenth of the rain ever shows up as the storm’s response. Where did the rest go, what exactly rose in the creek, and how do we describe that rise so precisely that two hydrologists in different countries mean the same thing by “lag”?

1 · The unit of study

The watershed and its network

Everything in this Part happens inside one shape: the watershed (drainage basin, catchment) — the area that topographically appears to send all its water through a chosen cross section of a stream, the outlet. Its boundary is the divide; its horizontal projection is the drainage area. The statement that organizes the chapter is deceptively simple: watershed geology, soils, topography, and land cover determine the magnitude, timing, and quality of the water entering the stream network and of the streamflow leaving the watershed. Inside the divide the water is collected by a hierarchical stream network, and Strahler’s (1952) rule for describing it fits in one breath: a stream with no tributaries is first order; two first-order streams meeting start a second-order stream; two second-order streams make a third — and a stream’s order does not change when it receives a tributary of lower order. At a map scale of 1:25,000 the Mississippi reaches its mouth as a twelfth-order stream. Horton (1945) and Schumm (1956) noticed that the numbers, lengths, and areas of streams of successive orders fall on nearly straight lines on semi-log paper — the “laws” of drainage-network composition, really typical ranges of statistical averages:

RB = Nω/Nω+1 ≈ 3–5   RL = Lω+1/Lω ≈ 1.5–3.5   RA = Aω+1/Aω ≈ 3–6bifurcation, length, and area ratios (Table 10.1); the exponential forms Nω = KN exp(−kNω) etc. have RB = exp(kN)

One more number describes how thoroughly a network dissects its area. The drainage density D = ΣL/A (km of channel per km2) runs from under 2 km−1 in permeable or arid terrain to over 100 km−1 in badlands, and simple geometry turns it into distances: the average stream spacing is about 1/D, the divide-to-stream distance (the hillslope length) about 1/(2D), and the average distance a drop travels to reach a channel about 1/(4D). 2 · The vocabulary

Anatomy of a storm hydrograph

Input is measured as a hyetograph (a histogram of water input, Pi, per time step); output as a hydrograph, the continuous record of discharge q(t) at the outlet. A stream responding to an isolated storm shows a characteristic shape: a relatively rapid rise to a well-defined peak, then a slower recession back toward the pre-event flow — the trace of a flood wave passing the gauge. Small watersheds (below about 50 km2) answer mostly with their hillslope travel times; in larger ones the travel time within the channel network takes over, and as the wave moves down the Sleepers River past gauges of 0.5, 8, 43, and 111 km2 the lecture-text figure shows it lagging, smoothing, and losing its multiple peaks. To compare storms and watersheds we need precise words. Dingman’s Table 10.2 names seven instants — tp0, tpc, tpe for the beginning, centroid, and end of effective input; tq0, tpk, tqc, tqe for the beginning, peak, centroid, and end of the response — and eight durations built from them:

TP = tpe − tp0   TLR = tq0 − tp0   Tr = tpk − tq0   TLP = tpk − tp0   TLPC = tpk − tpc   TLC = tqc − tpc   Tb = tqe − tq0   Tc = tqe − tpeinput duration, response lag, time of rise, lag-to-peak, centroid lag-to-peak, centroid lag, time base, time of concentration (Dingman Table 10.2); commonly TLPC ≈ 0.6 Tc

The centroid of a hyetograph is its balance point in time, tpc = ΣPi*ti/ΣPi* (Eq. 10.13); the centroid lag between input and response centroids turns out to be the theoretically meaningful response time of a watershed, though the centroid lag-to-peak is used more often. The time of concentration is the time for water from the hydraulically most distant point to reach the outlet; if effective rain lasted that long at a constant rate, outflow would equal inflow and the hydrograph would flatten into equilibrium runoff — something parking lots achieve and natural watersheds almost never do, which is why real peaks are instantaneous and qpk < p* Ac. 3 · The separation

Which part of the river is the storm?

Watershed W-3 of the Sleepers River Research Watershed in Vermont (8.37 km2) received P = 38 mm on 18 September 1973. The creek was already flowing before the rain and kept flowing for days afterward, so some of the rise must be water from earlier storms and some of this storm’s water will leave long after the obvious response. The practical question — how do we identify the portion of streamflow associated with a given event? — has no scientific answer, only a conventional one: draw a line. Base flow is whatever sits below it (presumed to come from persistent, slowly varying sources, mainly ground water); event flow (direct runoff, storm runoff, quickflow) is whatever sits above, and q(t) = q*(t) + qBF(t). The lines are heuristic: Dingman’s Box 10.2 uses a separation line rising from the start of the hydrograph rise at 0.000547 m3 s−1 per km2 per hour until it meets the recession; others use a horizontal line, or a fixed number of days after the peak. Perform the separation with each method, integrate the event flow, and divide by the watershed area to get Q* as a depth. Then build the effective hyetograph P* (the box scales each hour’s rain by a constant so that P* = Q*), locate its centroid, and measure the centroid lag-to-peak.

A convenient fiction. Graphical separation has no direct scientific basis — it does not identify water from different sources (tracer studies in Chapter 2 show that much “event flow” is old water pushed out of storage). It is a bookkeeping device that lets us relate response to watershed properties. Two rules follow: use a consistent separation scheme within a study, and never claim that the line tells you where the water came from.

The ratio Q*/P for individual events is usually well below 0.5 and commonly below 0.1: for W-3 it is 0.098. It also varies enormously. Over four summers on a 1.8-km2 Alaskan watershed it ranged from 0.03 to 0.42, rising with the rain of previous storms; at Barrow, two storms of similar size gave Q*/P = 0.63 in a wet summer and 0.012 in a dry one — a fifty-fold difference from antecedent wetness alone. Across the southeastern United States (Woodruff and Hewlett 1970) long-term event flow is a small fraction of precipitation and less than half of total streamflow, so more than half of a river typically arrives by delayed routes as base flow. A forecast that gets Q*/P slightly wrong gets the flood peak badly wrong.

4 · The simplest model

The watershed as a leaky bucket

Strip a watershed to two ideas — conservation of mass and a storage that drains in proportion to how full it is — and you have the linear reservoir (Box 10.3): dS*/dt = p* − q* and q* = S*/T*. Combine them and the response to a constant effective input beginning at t = 0 is a rising exponential, and once input stops at TP the recession is a falling one:

q* = p* [1 − exp(−t/T*)] for t ≤ TP    q* = q*pk exp[−(t − TP)/T*] for t ≥ TPEqs. 10B3.5–10B3.7; the peak q*pk = p*[1 − exp(−TP/T*)] occurs exactly when input ceases; time to equilibrium (99 % of inflow) Teq = −ln(0.01) T* = 4.605 T*

The single parameter T*, the response time, integrates everything that controls travel time to the outlet: size (larger basins, larger T*), soils and geology (clay soils that shed water as overland flow respond faster than sandy soils that absorb it), slope (steeper, faster), land use (forests slow water; pavement and storm sewers hurry it), and wetness (a wet basin has a smaller T* than the same basin dry). The model preserves continuity (Q* = P*), its centroid lag equals T*, its time of rise equals the input duration, its recession decays with constant T*, and its time of concentration is Tc = 4.605 T* (Eq. 10.16). Paint a hyetograph below (or load the text’s cases: one unit of input in 1 h, in 4.5 h, front-loaded, back-loaded), set T*, and watch which features of the response depend on the watershed and which on the storm.

Reading Table 10.3 off the screen. Centroid lag and time of concentration depend only on T* — they are properties of the watershed. Time of rise and time base depend on the input duration as well; lag-to-peak and peak discharge depend on the exact timing of the inputs too. That is why the peak — the number flood forecasters care most about — is the hardest to pin to a watershed alone.

Chapter 2 · Identification of runoff sources and event-flow-generation processes

Paths to the Stream

Robert Horton, the founder of quantitative hydrology, taught a generation that storm runoff is rain that fails to infiltrate and runs over the surface. He was right about parking lots and deserts. He was wrong about nearly every forested hillslope in Georgia — where you can stand in a downpour and see no water moving on the ground at all, while the creek below rises steadily, mostly with water that fell before the storm began.

1 · Four doors

The hillslope runoff machine

Dingman’s Table 10.4 lists the mechanisms by which rain reaches a stream fast enough to count as event response. Channel precipitation falls straight into the channel: channels are rarely 1 % of the drainage area, yet because the water needs no travel time this source can be 5 % of event flow and up to 40 % of the peak on a small basin (Eqs. 10.22–10.24). Hortonian overland flow (infiltration excess) occurs when and where the input rate exceeds the surface saturated conductivity and the storm outlasts the time of ponding; the overland-flow rate is then qho = p − f(t) and grows during a steady storm as infiltration decays toward Kh. Saturation overland flow (Dunne overland flow, saturation excess) occurs where the water table rises to the surface from below — near streams, in hollows, over shallow impeding layers — after which every drop landing on the saturated area runs off regardless of intensity, joined by return flow breaking out of the ground upslope. And subsurface storm flow arrives through the ground: from near-stream ground-water mounds, from the sudden pressurization of the capillary fringe, from perched layers on a sloping slab, and through macropores. Run the machine: set the storm, the surface, the water table, and the slope, and watch which doors open.

Tp = Kh |ψf| (φ − θ0) / [p (p − Kh)]time of ponding for a constant input rate p > Kh (Eq. 10.25, from the Green–Ampt model of Part 04): larger wetting-front suction and drier soil delay ponding; more intense rain hastens it
2 · Will it pond?

Rain against conductivity

Figure 10.25 (Freeze 1972) settles an old argument with one plot: the range of natural rainfall intensities overlaps the saturated conductivity of clays, silts, and loams, but falls far below that of sands and gravels — and the surface horizons of most vegetated soils conduct better than the mineral soil beneath them, because of roots, worms, and organic matter. So Hortonian overland flow is the rule only where rain is intense and surfaces are tight: semiarid and arid land with sparse cover, frozen or compacted or trampled ground, and impermeable surfaces. Betson’s (1964) partial-area concept refines Horton: where overland flow does occur it comes from a limited, low-permeability fraction of the basin (4.6–46 % on Appalachian farm watersheds) that stays fairly constant from storm to storm. Slide the rainfall intensity across the ladder of materials and see which ones can pond; then compute a time of ponding.

3 · Saturation from below

The variable source area

In humid regions streams are gaining, the water table meets the stream surface, and near the channel it lies close to the ground. Recharge during a storm raises it; where it reaches the surface, infiltration stops and saturation overland flow begins. Thomas Dunne’s field work in Vermont (Dunne and Black 1970) made this the central mechanism of humid-region storm response and established the variable source area concept: the saturated fraction of a watershed expands and contracts with its wetness — seasonally, and during a single storm — which is why Q*/P swings so widely. Saturation also appears away from streams wherever subsurface flow converges faster than it can drain: in hillslope hollows, below concave slope breaks, where the soil thins, or above a layer whose conductivity drops with depth. Beven and Kirkby (1979) compressed that intuition into the topographic index, the engine of TOPMODEL:

TIi = ln(ai / tan βi)    di = d̄ + M (TĪ − TIi)Eqs. 10.27–10.28: ai is the area draining to a pixel per unit contour length, βi its slope; a pixel saturates when its storage deficit di reaches zero — large contributing area and flat ground first

Explore the hillside: the map is a synthetic hollow; drag the watershed-mean deficit down (wetter) and watch the saturated area grow outward from the channel and the hollow; probe any pixel for its a, slope, and TI.

4 · Water through the ground

Interflow, macropores, and the sloping slab

Water in the unsaturated zone is under tension and cannot step directly into a stream, whose water is at or above atmospheric pressure: the unsaturated zone cannot be a direct source of streamflow. What it can do is feed saturated zones that are. On a homogeneous slope, modeled Darcian flow stays nearly vertical during a storm and only turns downslope afterward — too slowly to make a peak, though Hewlett and Hibbert’s famous 15-m soil trough on a 40° slope drained for 145 days and showed that unsaturated downslope flow can sustain recessions. Fast subsurface response needs something else: macropores (root channels, burrows, cracks, 3–100 mm across, mostly in the top 15 cm) carrying water millimeters per second through otherwise unsaturated soil — Ward’s thatched roof, which stays dry inside because straw aligned downslope sheds every drop along itself — or a sloping slab: a permeable layer over an impeding one, in which a perched saturated wedge builds and drains downslope. Beven’s (1981) kinematic-wave solution for the slab is tidy enough to work by hand (Box 10.5), and its recession is exactly the linear reservoir of Chapter 1 with T* = X(φ − θfc)/(2Kh tan β). Solve the box along for a 30-m sandy slope at 32° hit by 25 mm/h for an hour, then turn the sliders.

5 · Which mechanism, where?

The Dunne diagram, old water, and the tracer evidence

Dunne (1978) summarized the field evidence on one diagram: thin soils, low permeability, gentle slopes, arid climates, and sparse vegetation push a landscape toward Hortonian overland flow; deep, permeable soils on steep slopes under dense humid-climate vegetation toward subsurface storm flow; and the gentle, concave, near-stream ground of humid basins toward saturation overland flow. Mirus and Loague (2013) tested it with 140 simulations on four real watersheds: rainfall depth drives the shift from subsurface storm flow to saturation overland flow, rainfall intensity the occurrence of Hortonian flow, and saturated conductivity matters most of all. How do we know any of this? Because water carries a chemical signature of where it has been. End-member mixing analysis (EMMA) measures a conservative tracer (chloride, silica, the stable isotopes 18O and 2H) in the stream and in each candidate source and solves the two mass balances:

q = q1 + q2   c q = c1q1 + c2q2  ⇒  q2/q = (c − c1)/(c2 − c1)two-component hydrograph separation (Eqs. 10.18–10.20); it works only if the end members differ in concentration and the tracer is conservative

The answer, in forest after forest, is that storm peaks are largely old water — pre-event water displaced from storage by the new rain (Table 10.5). Sort the field situations onto the Dunne diagram, then run the mixing calculator.

Chapter 3 · Channel processes, measurement, and the streamflow record (PBA 3.1–3.4)

Reading the River

Nobody measures discharge. A gauge measures a water level, a hydrographer in waders measures velocities and depths a few dozen times a year, and everything the USGS publishes as “streamflow” is a calculation stitched between those two. Learn how the stitching is done and a twenty-year record becomes a character study of a watershed — its floods, its droughts, and its memory.

1 · The measurement

Velocity times area, strip by strip

Stand in the Wet Rip River at a stage of 0.9 m with a tape and a current meter. Divide the cross section into vertical strips (troughs) of equal width; in each, measure the depth and the velocity — at 0.6 of the depth below the surface in shallow water, or the average of readings at 0.2 and 0.8 of the depth where it is deeper, because velocity falls toward the bed and the banks; multiply velocity by depth by width to get the strip’s discharge; and add the strips. That is the velocity–area method, and for a single gauging the errors in velocity, depth, and width combine to roughly ±5–10 % (PBA 3.1). Arrange the steps in order (the lecture’s poll), then hover the strips of profile #1 to see each term and the sum.

Q = Σ Vi di Δwimid-section velocity–area method; Vi = mean velocity in the vertical, di depth, Δwi strip width
2 · The translation

The rating curve and its cliff

Repeat the gauging at four stages and plot discharge against stage: the stage–discharge rating curve. On log–log axes it is nearly straight, so it is fitted as a power law Q = a hb, with an exponent of 2–3 for a roughly parabolic channel because wetted area grows faster than depth. From then on the gauge needs only a stage recorder — this is how 8,705 USGS stations turn a water level into a published discharge. Inside the measured range the curve interpolates well; beyond it, it extrapolates, and the error grows fast. Worst of all is bank-full: once the river spills onto its floodplain the geometry changes completely, the wetted area jumps, and a power law fitted to in-channel flow has nothing to say. Drag the stage and watch the prediction and its warnings; the lecture’s poll question about a 3.0-m stage is waiting at the top of the floodplain.

3 · The wave

Flood waves outrun the water

In the channel the hydrograph is a wave, and waves have their own rules. One-dimensional open-channel flow obeys conservation of mass, ∂Q/∂x = −∂A/∂t + qL (lateral inflow raises discharge downstream; overbank outflow lowers it), and a momentum equation whose driving gradient is the channel slope plus the water-surface slope: on the rising limb depth decreases downstream, so the flow is pushed faster than slope alone would; on the falling limb it is held back. The net effect is to flatten and delay the wave as it travels. Approximating the flow as uniform gives Manning’s equation, and a short derivation from continuity gives the surprise:

u = (ε/nM) y2/3 ζ1/2    U = (1/w) ∂Q/∂y = 1.5 uuniform-flow velocity (Eqs. 10.43–10.44, ε = 1 m1/3 s−1) and the flood-wave (kinematic) celerity (Eqs. 10.45–10.48): the wave travels one and a half times faster than the water

A flood crest therefore overtakes the water that formed it — the water at the crest is continually being replaced. When the flood overtops the banks, the slow overbank flow dilutes the celerity: U = 1.5 uC (wC/w) (Eq. 10.50), so a floodplain three times the channel width makes the wave slower than the channel water itself. Floodplains store water and lower peaks — nature’s detention basins. Set a channel, release a wave, and race a floating leaf against the crest.

4 · The routing

Convex routing through a reach

Streamflow routing predicts the outflow hydrograph of a reach from its inflow hydrograph and hydraulics. The simplest practical scheme, the convex method, treats the reach as a linear reservoir with travel time T* = X/U, picks a time step Δt ≤ T*, defines a routing coefficient CX = Δt/T*, and marches:

QOt+1 = CX · QIt + (1 − CX) · QOtEq. 10.56; CX = 1 is pure translation, smaller CX (longer travel times) flattens and delays the peak; flatter slopes, shallower flow, and rougher channels raise the delay

Dingman’s Box 10.6 routes a triangular flood (peak 36 m3/s, rising for 1 h and receding for 4 h) through a 2,500-m reach 20 m wide with slope 0.0005 and nM = 0.05, computing the travel time at three-quarters of the peak inflow. Solve it along — depth, velocity, celerity, travel time, coefficient — then route any flood you like and see the family of outflows for different CX.

5 · The record

Twenty years of three Georgia rivers

Now read a real record. The USGS gauges on the Middle Oconee near Athens (02217500), the North Oconee at Athens (02217770), and Peachtree Creek in Atlanta (02336300) have daily mean discharges from 2005 to 2025 — the records of PBA 3.3 and 3.4. Three readings extract a watershed’s character from such a record. The recession after a storm, fitted as Q(t) = Q0 eBt (a linear reservoir draining with no input, T* = −1/B), tells how well the basin stores and releases water and how long it would take to “dry up” to a target flow: t = ln(Q*/Q0)/B. The flow-duration curve plots discharge against the percentage of time it is equaled or exceeded (Weibull position P = 100 M/(n + 1) for rank M of n days): a steep high-flow end means a flashy flood regime, a flat low-flow end means drought resilience; Q95 is a dependable low flow, Q10 a high flow, Q50 the median. And the Richards–Baker flashiness index (Baker et al. 2004) is the total path length of day-to-day oscillations divided by total flow:

RB = Σ|qi − qi−1| / Σqidimensionless; below 0.25 stable and groundwater-dominated, 0.25–0.45 moderate, 0.45–0.65 moderately flashy, above 0.65 highly flashy and runoff-dominated

Pick a station and a tab. Zoom on Hurricane Helene (late September 2024), fit its recession, read the duration curve, and compare the flashiness of a forested Piedmont river with that of an urban creek.

Chapter 4 · Rainfall–runoff modeling

Predicting the Flood

A culvert must be sized before the storm that will test it has formed. The engineer’s way out is to abandon physics for bookkeeping: treat the watershed as a black box that turns an input hyetograph into an output hydrograph, calibrate the box on storms you have, and trust it on the storm you have not seen. The methods are old, crude, and everywhere — and each hides its biggest uncertainty in the same place.

1 · The systems view

Design floods, forecasts, and the losses

Two different jobs share the same models. A design flood is a flood of a specified probability — the 10-year flood for a small culvert, the 25-year for a bridge, the 100-year or the probable maximum flood for a dam spillway — generated by feeding a design rainfall of that return period through the model, with a duration near the watershed’s time of concentration. A flood forecast estimates the actual flood from a storm in progress, for warnings and reservoir operation. Either way, the systems approach (Fig. 10.47) deals only with effective precipitation P* and event flow Q*, so the first task is always to decide what fraction of the rain becomes event flow: P* = P − losses, where the losses are evapotranspiration during the storm (small), canopy storage (about 1 mm per unit of leaf-area index, filled quickly), depression storage (5–10 mm on turf), and the filling of soil storage — so P*/P is set largely by antecedent wetness. Four conceptual loss models are in common use (Fig. 10.49). Apply each to the same hyetograph.

Where the uncertainty lives. Because P* is usually a small fraction of P, a small error in the losses is a large relative error in P* — and therefore in the peak. The validity of the P* estimate matters at least as much as the transfer function that follows it. Keep that sentence in mind through the next three sections.
2 · One line

The rational method

For small urban basins the oldest method still rules: the peak discharge is proportional to rainfall intensity and drainage area, with a runoff coefficient CR that depends on land use — 0.05 for a flat sandy lawn, 0.95 for roofs and pavement (Table 10.9). The intensity is that of a design storm whose duration equals the time of concentration (so that the whole basin contributes at the peak), and the return period of the peak is taken as the return period of the rain. Its sole parameter is also its sole weakness: CR varies from storm to storm with antecedent conditions, and there are rarely data to pin it down.

qpk = εR CR AD p*Eq. 10.65 with εR = 0.278 for qpk in m3/s, AD in km2, and p* in mm/h
3 · The method everyone uses

The SCS curve-number method

The most widely used rainfall–runoff model in the United States began with the Soil Conservation Service and lives on in every drainage manual because it runs on information the NRCS maps everywhere: a hydrologic soil group (A, fast-draining sands, to D, clays and shallow soils over rock; Table 10.10), a land cover and its condition, and from these a curve number CN (Table 10.11) that sets the watershed’s maximum retention Smax. The method assumes that an initial abstraction SI = 0.2 Smax must be satisfied before any runoff, and that thereafter retention and runoff share the rain in proportion to how much of Smax remains:

Smax = 25,400/CN − 254 (mm)    P* = (P − 0.2 Smax)2 / (P + 0.8 Smax)    Tr = 0.5 TP* + 0.6 Tc    qpk = 0.208 P* AD/TrEqs. 10.66–10.71: P* and Smax in mm, AD in km2, times in h, qpk in m3/s; the peak assumes a triangular hydrograph with time base 2.67 Tr, and Tc comes from an empirical formula (Table 10.8)

Curve numbers are tabulated for average antecedent wetness (condition II) and adjusted for dry (I) or wet (III) conditions by the rain of the previous five days (Table 10.12). Box 10.10 applies the method to 107 mm of rain in 3.4 h on a 3.21-km2 watershed of forest and meadow. Solve it along — and when you reach the wet-condition row, check the book’s own arithmetic before you believe it. The calculator that unlocks afterward lets you compose any watershed from the full table, compare five time-of-concentration formulas, and draw the SCS dimensionless unit hydrograph.

Use it, but do not confuse it with hydrology. Tests against measured storms are mixed: on an Iowa research watershed the method’s a priori P* bore little relation to the separated event flow (Fig. 10.52). It persists because it is simple, uses data that exist, is packaged in every manual, gives “reasonable” results, and has few practicable rivals. Klemeš warned against mistaking the manipulation of curve numbers for the science of hydrology; field observation of a basin’s rise times is always advisable.
4 · The transfer function

The unit hydrograph

The TP*-hour unit hydrograph of a watershed is its event-flow response to one unit of effective rainfall (1 inch or 1 cm) applied at a constant rate for TP* hours. Its central hypothesis is linearity: twice the effective rain gives twice the ordinates, and inputs in successive periods simply add. Three constructions follow. Scale: multiply the unit graph by P* to forecast a storm. Lag and add: a TP*-hour unit graph added to itself lagged by TP* is the response to two units in 2TP* hours, so halving it gives the 2TP*-hour unit graph. S-curve: add unit graphs lagged by TP* indefinitely and the sum levels off into the S-hydrograph, the response to rain of infinite duration; subtract it from itself lagged by any shorter interval, rescale, and you have the unit graph for that interval — including, in the limit, the instantaneous unit hydrograph. Dunne and Leopold’s recipe builds one from four or five observed storms of similar duration, each separated, divided by its own P*, averaged, and adjusted until the area is one unit of runoff. Work the box’s 2.5-h unit graph: forecast 2.4 in, derive the 5-h and 1-h graphs, convolve your own storm — and audit the figure’s volume.

Why the method survives its own assumptions. Freeze (1972) saw no physical reason for watersheds to respond linearly, and they do not: contributing areas change, response times vary with wetness, and small storms are far less linear than large ones. Yet Dunne and Leopold reckoned unit-hydrograph peaks are usually within 25 % of the truth — about as good as the data allow — and the geomorphologic unit hydrograph (Eqs. 10.73–10.74), built from the network ratios of Chapter 1, offers a route for ungauged basins. The linear assumption is most defensible for large storms on large basins, when storage is full.
