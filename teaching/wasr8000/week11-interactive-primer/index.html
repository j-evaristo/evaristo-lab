Unscrambling the Omelette

An interactive primer on what happens when tracers are asked to take a mixture apart. One equation — a stream, a well, or a tree is a weighted average of its sources — runs through all four readings: the storm hydrograph that turned out to be mostly old water, the swamp that turned out not to be sealed from the aquifer beneath it, the trees whose hydrogen told a different story from their oxygen, and the seasonal isotope cycle that cannot tell you a catchment's mean transit time but can tell you, to within a few percent, how much of its streamflow is younger than about two to three months. Builds on Weeks 2, 3, 5 and 10; no tracer background needed.

1 · The opening question

The storm that carried almost no rain

A summer storm drops 40 mm on a steep, forested catchment. Within an hour the stream has risen fivefold; within three it peaks. Hydrology textbooks of the 1960s had a clear picture of what that peak was made of: rain that fell on the land and ran, over the surface or through shallow soil pipes, straight into the channel. Then, beginning in 1969, hydrologists started measuring the water itself — its tritium, then its oxygen-18 and deuterium — instead of only its pressure. Klaus and McDonnell's review opens with what they found and what it did to the textbooks. If you could sample the stream at the very peak, what would you expect the water to be?

This is the week's whole argument in miniature. A tracer separation is only as good as the end-members you choose, the assumptions you make about them, and the error bars you are willing to put on the answer. Sections 2–7 take the two-component separation apart and put it back together with honest uncertainty; Sections 8–10 run the same equation through tree stems and a swamp; Sections 11–15 replace storms with seasons and discover which quantity a damped seasonal cycle can and cannot measure.

2 · Two buckets, one stream

The mass-balance lever

The whole of isotope hydrograph separation (IHS) fits in three lines. Streamflow is the sum of pre-event water — water stored in the catchment before the rain began — and event water from the storm itself (eq. 1). Tracer mass is conserved, so the stream's tracer value is the flow-weighted average of the two (eq. 2). Solve for the pre-event fraction and you have eq. 3: the stream's value, measured as a distance along the line between the two end-members. Nothing about flow paths, velocities or mechanisms enters — which is the method's power and, as the review argues, the reason it stopped generating new process insight once pre-event water dominance became "a given". Words first. Pre-event water (often called old water) is water stored in the catchment before the streamflow-generating event; event water (new water) is the water from the current precipitation event — rain, or during snowmelt the melt water from the snowpack, with pre-event water being what sat below the snowpack when the experiment began. The equations are constrained so that Ct lies between Cp and Ce and the flows lie between zero and Qt; when a result breaks that constraint, Section 4 shows, the problem is usually an end-member you have not counted. And the vocabulary matters later: Kirchner's "young water" of Sections 12–15 is not event water, and he says so in his abstract.
3 · Five assumptions

Five ways to be wrong, and twenty years of finding them

Sklash and co-workers (1976) and Sklash and Farvolden (1979) wrote down the assumptions behind eqs. 1–3 — four at first, later extended to five (Moore, 1989; Buttle, 1994). The review's central claim is that "the current limitations associated with IHS are the same issues of the 1970s": not the instruments, which have improved beyond recognition, but the assumptions, which several papers "continue to gloss-over". Click each assumption for what the last two decades have found, and how the field has tried to patch it.

The review's verdict, assumption by assumption: the first "is still a rather critical factor for any IHS"; the fifth is "rather obvious" (and can even be turned to advantage, to quantify wetland and lake contributions); the second still hides a large, mostly unquantified source of uncertainty in the spatial variability of the input; the third "continues to challenge current work, with few if any studies quantifying these patterns"; and for the fourth "a better assumption is that soil water contributes significantly to channel stormflow unless there is evidence to the contrary". Section 4 shows what that last sentence does to a separation.

When two isotopes give two answers

Why the two isotopes disagree. A two-component separation sees only the line from groundwater to event water. Soil water at (−2, −23) is off that line, so each isotope projects it differently: by δ²H, a litre of pure soil water is counted as 56 % pre-event ((−23 + 14)/(−30 + 14)); by δ¹⁸O, its −2 ‰ lies beyond the event water's −3 ‰, so it counts as minus 50 % pre-event — and the δ¹⁸O-based pre-event flow dips below baseflow at the peak, the "unrealistic component mixtures that sometimes exceed 100% or fall below 0%" that the review says pushed the field toward three-component separations. Put the soil water back on the meteoric line (the preset) and the two isotopes agree again; set its share to zero and both match the truth.
5 · Two tracers, three sources

The mixing triangle

Each extra source needs an extra tracer, or an extra piece of knowledge. Eqs. 4–5 extend the mass balance to n components; with three, "either one of the flow components must be known or an additional tracer must be used". DeWalle and colleagues (1988) knew one: they assumed event water reached the stream only as direct precipitation on the channel, an area that itself grows with discharge. Wels and colleagues (1991) added silicate; Ogunkoya and Jenkins (1993) added chloride to deuterium, "the most popular approach in recent years". The picture that makes this work is a bivariate mixing diagram: if the stream samples fall inside the triangle of the three end-members, the sources bound the mixture; if they fall outside, something is missing. The triangle below is Bazemore and co-workers' (1994) δ¹⁸O–chloride space, reprinted as the review's Fig. 2, with the end-members read off their plot. End-member mixing analysis, in one paragraph

The triangle is the smallest end-member mixing analysis (EMMA). With more tracers the picture generalises: Hooper and co-workers (1990) — "the foundational end-member mixing analysis (EMMA) work" the review cites — and Christophersen and Hooper (1992), the EMMA paper on this week's further-reading list, ask how many dimensions the stream chemistry actually occupies (a k-dimensional mixing subspace can be explained by k + 1 end-members), which candidate waters bound the stream samples in that subspace, and how well the mixture reproduces each tracer. Hooper's (2003) diagnostic tools test the assumptions the same way Section 4 did: a tracer that is not conservative, or an end-member that is missing, leaves structured residuals. Two warnings the review adds from its own compilation: the computed proportions are tracer-specific — Rice and Hornberger (1998) found differences above 50 % between tracer combinations for several storms — and solutes "are not truly conservative", so a solute tracer may be labelling a flow path (silicate) rather than a time source (chloride, the isotopes). The EMMA papers are further reading, not among this week's four readings; what is said of them here is background.

7 · Error bars that mean something

Genereux's propagation

A fraction computed from three measured numbers inherits their uncertainty, and the review traces how the field learned to say so: Rodhe (1981) first, varying groundwater by ±0.5 ‰ and melt water by ±1 ‰ and finding ±15 % in the groundwater contribution; Monte Carlo sampling by Bazemore and colleagues (50,000 calculations per stream sample); and Genereux's (1998) Gaussian error propagation, "now the state-of-the-art approach". Joerin and co-workers (2002) drew a line that matters for your term papers: statistical uncertainty (end-member variability and laboratory error) is what everyone propagates; model uncertainty (violated assumptions) is what nearly everyone ignores. The explorer below is the Genereux formula as it appears, in identical form, in both Evaristo papers (their eq. 4): the uncertainty in a two-source fraction from the uncertainties of the two end-members and of the mixture.

Wf = { [ (1 − f) WA / ΔC ]² + [ f WB / ΔC ]² + [ Wmix / ΔC ]² }½,   ΔC = |CB − CA|Genereux (1998); eq. 4 in both Evaristo papers

Here f is the fraction of source B in the mixture, WA and WB the uncertainties assigned to the two end-members (a standard deviation, a standard error, or a laboratory precision — the choice is yours, and it is the biggest decision in the calculation), and Wmix the uncertainty of the sample itself. The form above is the published one rewritten with (CB − Cmix) = (1 − f)ΔC and (Cmix − CA) = fΔC.

Three things the curve teaches. It is concave up when the end-member uncertainties dominate: the fraction is best known near the middle, worst at the ends, because the end-member you depend on most is the one whose error you carry. Everything scales as 1/ΔC: doubling the separation between sources halves every error bar, which is why the Christchurch paper's second curve (ΔC = 5) sits at roughly half the height of the first (11 % to 5 %). And when end-members are nearly indistinguishable, ΔC → 0 and the error bars blow up — assumption 1 of Section 3, written as arithmetic. The review's list of error sources, from Uhlenbrook and Hoeg's (2003) three-component study: tracer and discharge measurement, intra-storm variability of δ¹⁸O, the elevation effect, mineral dissolution during runoff, and the spatial variability of tracer concentrations — the one they single out as "the most critical", and one that targeted or intensive sampling can shrink.
8 · The same lever, underground

Thirty-five trees in a botanic garden

Replace the stream with a tree stem and the two end-members with soil water and groundwater, and eq. 3 becomes eq. 2 of Evaristo, McDonnell and Clemens (2017): the fraction of groundwater in xylem water. Root uptake is generally taken to be non-fractionating, so xylem water should be an honest average of what the roots drank. The Christchurch Botanic Gardens offered an unusually clean test: 35 tree species on flat, uniform fine sandy loam, a soil profile whose isotopes did not differ with depth (p > .10, so the ~1 m of soil can be one compartment), and a groundwater end-member from a 175 m bore. Soil water averaged −52 to −53.5 ‰ δ²H and −6.5 to −6.6 ‰ δ¹⁸O; groundwater −62 ± 0.8 and −9.1 ± 0.6. Then the two isotopes disagreed — and the disagreement had a direction. The direction of the error is the lesson. Groundwater is lighter than soil water in both isotopes, so anything that makes xylem δ²H lighter — and uptake fractionation does exactly that, by 4.7 to 5.6 ‰ on average here, in 12 of 18 and 15 of 17 trees — looks, to a δ²H-only mass balance, like more groundwater. Hence "an apparent overestimation of groundwater contribution to xylem" and the paper's rule: if Δ²H is positive, solve eq. 2 with δ¹⁸O. The Bayesian mixing model (SIAR, 500,000 iterations, uniform prior, both isotopes) lands between the two single-isotope answers and, the authors argue, is less sensitive to the fractionation. Its uncertainty behaves differently too: their Fig. 5 shows the mass-balance curve concave up (Section 7's shape; the page's reading of that figure is that it was drawn with ΔC = 2.5 and uncertainties of about 0.19 ‰ on each term — the inputs are not stated) but the Bayesian curve concave down, least certain when the two sources are nearly equal. A press-on point for the discussant: the text says δ²H gave a groundwater contribution "1.2 times greater than when using δ¹⁸O", which cannot be reproduced from the site means in the table above (52 vs 1 %, 68 vs 28 %); ask what the 1.2 averages over.
9 · Not so isolated

The Okefenokee's fingerprint in the Floridan Aquifer

The Okefenokee Swamp — 1,500–1,800 km² of peat prairies and cypress on the Georgia–Florida line, about 1,300 mm of rain a year — was long presumed sealed from the Upper Floridan Aquifer beneath it by the Miocene Hawthorne Group, a clay-rich confining unit up to ~70 m thick. Rykiel's 1977 budget lost about 80 % of the rain to evapotranspiration and sent the remaining 20 % out as surface flow, so there seemed to be nothing left to leak; and the swamp sits several metres above the aquifer's potentiometric surface, with a head difference of up to 21 m, so if a path existed the water could only go down. Evaristo, Jackson and Rasmussen (2025) tested the seal with the same lever as Section 2: swamp water, evaporatively enriched, as one end-member (δ¹⁸O −1.7 ± 0.58, n = 4); regional aquifer water from Clark and colleagues' 1997 wells on two flowpaths as the other (−3.86 ± 0.43, n = 15); and wells and municipal tap waters drawn from the aquifer beneath and around the swamp as the mixtures. The paper's reading: the Hawthorne is a semi-confining unit, not a seal, and 5–15 % of the rain leaves the swamp downward.
Why the two isotopes disagree here too — the page's arithmetic, offered for the discussant. The beneath-swamp mean (−2.6, −9.8) has a deuterium excess of 11.0 ‰; the swamp end-member has 6.6 and the regional end-member 8.3. A mixture of two waters cannot have a higher d-excess than both of them, so the mean sample lies above the mixing line (a two-member mixture with the δ¹⁸O fraction 0.58 would have δ²H ≈ −13.5, not −9.8), and δ²H duly returns a larger fraction (0.82) than δ¹⁸O (0.58). That is Section 4's lesson in a confined aquifer: either an end-member is not what its mean says (four swamp samples from two months in 2025 standing in for the water that recharged over years; a 1997 regional end-member measured by a different method with ±1 ‰ precision in δ²H), or something is missing. The paper itself says δ¹⁸O gives the "more conservative" estimate and lists seasonal shifts and a minor paleowater component among its uncertainties. Three more things to press on: the LCE is printed as [δ²H − a δ¹⁸O − b]/S with S never defined, and the raw offsets of the swamp mean (−4.1 ‰) and the regional mean (−4.3 ‰) from that LMWL are nearly the same, so the −42 vs ≈ −5 contrast in Fig. 2C must come from S and from the individual samples; Section 4.3 says δ¹⁸O increases "with distance from the coast" and cites Fig. 2(E), where Section 3.1 and the figure itself show it increasing toward the coast in Fig. 2(F); and the leakage is "130–220 mm yr⁻¹" in Section 4.1 and Fig. 4 but "5–15 % of annual rainfall (~50–150 mm)" in Sections 4.4–4.5 — 130–220 mm of 1,300 is 10–17 %, and 5–15 % of 1,300 is 65–195 mm. Smaller still: Fig. 2C labels the swamp box n = 5 where the text has n = 4, and Fig. 3A's caption runs to July 2020 where the methods say August 2020.
10 · Pressure through the roof

A second, independent witness

Isotopes say swamp water is in the aquifer; they cannot say how fast the two are coupled. For that the paper turned to 1,135 days of daily water levels (July 2017 to August 2020) in two aquifer wells — 27G003 at Waycross and 27E004 at Jones Island — and in the Jones Island swamp. The two wells track each other almost perfectly (Pearson r = 0.980; dynamic-time-warping distance 69), the swamp only loosely (r = 0.54 and 0.51; DTW > 400), and Granger causality runs from Waycross to Jones Island with a one-day lag. The decisive step was to deconvolve the aquifer's step response to a change in swamp stage and fit it with the one-dimensional vertical-flow type curve of eq. 3: the response of a leaky layer of thickness b and vertical hydraulic diffusivity D. Matching the curve at the point (30 d, 0.5) gave D = 291 m² d⁻¹ with b = 151.8 m. Set D and b and watch the signal cross the roof.

11 · From storms to seasons

The damping machine

A storm separation asks what fraction of this week's flow is this week's rain. Kirchner's question is slower: how long, on average, does rain take to become streamflow? Precipitation isotopes swing through the year — heavy in summer, light in winter — and a catchment that stores and mixes water smooths that swing. Fig. 1 of the paper shows it at Fernow WS4, West Virginia: a precipitation cycle of roughly ±3.6 ‰ in δ¹⁸O (the page's reading of the fitted sinusoid) damped to about ±0.4 ‰ in baseflow and delayed by a season. If the transit-time distribution (TTD) is a gamma distribution with shape α and mean τ̄, the damping and the delay follow from its Fourier transform (eqs. 7–9), and inverting eq. 8 gives the mean transit time (eq. 10) — "the standard tool" when α = 1 is assumed. Three ways to read a tracer record, from the paper's Section 1: time-domain convolution (needs continuous input records spanning several times the MTT), spectral analysis (needs series spanning a wide range of timescales), and sine-wave fitting — the simplest member of both families, examining one frequency, and the only one that works on "sparse, irregularly sampled data sets". Its price is the assumption of a stationary, linear, time-invariant catchment (eq. 1 of the paper, the same convolution integral as Week 5's lumped-parameter models), with evapotranspiration ignored and the TTD's shape assumed. DeWalle and colleagues read Fernow's damping as a mean transit time of 1.4 years; the page's reading of the two amplitudes (about 0.4 and 3.6 ‰, a ratio near 0.11) returns 1.4 years by eq. 10 — the same answer, and, as Section 13 will show, probably wrong by a large factor either way.
12 · What the amplitude actually measures

The young water fraction

Here is the thought experiment at the heart of the paper. Imagine a catchment in which some fraction of the rain bypasses storage and reaches the stream at once, carrying the precipitation cycle intact, while the rest is stored and mixed for so long that its cycle is obliterated. The stream's amplitude ratio is then exactly the bypass fraction. Real TTDs are not that stark, but the intuition holds: the amplitude ratio is sensitive to transit times much shorter than the cycle's period and nearly blind to transit times much longer. So Kirchner defines the young water fraction Fyw as the share of the TTD younger than a threshold age τyw (eq. 13) and searches for the threshold at which Fyw matches AS/AP across all mean transit times. There is one: between 1.7 and 2.7 months for shape factors 0.5 to 1.5 (eq. 14), with a root-mean-square error below 0.023 across the whole range of ratios from 0 to 1. The threshold that makes it work (the paper's Fig. 10)

Why this is not a coincidence, in the paper's words: Fyw "has been defined with exactly this result in mind". The amplitude ratio and the young water fraction are both dimensionless, both run from 0 to 1, and both are governed by the short-transit-time part of the distribution; the threshold age just names the boundary the amplitude ratio was always drawing. The ambiguity it leaves — is "young" 1.75 or 2.27 months? — is the price of not knowing α, and Section 15 shows it is a small price. What you do not get, and the paper is explicit about this, is any idea how old the old water is.
13 · Two valleys, one gauge

The aggregation thought experiment

Now the trick the paper is named for. Suppose the gauged catchment is really two subcatchments — two valleys, two hillslopes, or two flow paths down the same slope — with different mean transit times, flowing together before the gauge. Each damps the precipitation cycle by its own eq. 8, and the stream carries the average of the two cycles. In the paper's example, valley 2 has a mean transit time of 0.1 years and transmits 85 % of the cycle; valley 1 has 4 years and transmits 4 %. Mixed one to one, their true mean transit time is 2.05 years and their combined amplitude ratio is 0.43 — but read that 0.43 off the single-catchment curve of eq. 10 and you infer 0.33 years, six times too short. The young water fractions, meanwhile, simply average. Drag the valleys apart and watch the two estimates diverge.

The mixed transit-time distribution (the paper's Fig. 6)

Fixed axes: transit time 0–2 years; density 0–2 yr⁻¹ (linear). The mixture of two exponentials is a hyperexponential — steep at first, then a long tail — and not a member of the gamma family at all. Some α would always reproduce this one mixture's true mean (for the Fig. 5 pair, α ≈ 0.2), but nothing in the data tells you which, and the paper's Fig. 14 shows that even estimating α from the phase leaves "a severe underestimation bias". (A press-on nit: the Fig. 6 caption assigns "1 and 0.1 years" to "the orange and red dashed lines, respectively" while the plot labels red as subcatchment 1 and orange as subcatchment 2.)

The rule of thumb behind the bias, from Kirchner and colleagues' 1993 acid-deposition work: aggregation error is proportional to the nonlinearity of the governing equation and to the variance of the heterogeneous parameter. Eq. 8 is strongly nonlinear exactly where real catchments live — mean transit times from fractions of a year to several years — and in a one-to-one mixture the amplitude ratio "will be determined primarily by the shorter of the two mean transit times". Note what averages and what does not: the amplitude ratios of the two valleys average almost exactly (the paper's Fig. 8, RMSE 0.001–0.018), the young water fractions average by definition, and the true mean transit time of the mixture is simply the share-weighted mean — but the mean transit time inferred from the mixed cycle by eq. 10 does not average, because it depends on the shape of the mixed distribution.
14 · A thousand synthetic catchments

The benchmark test, run here

Two valleys could be a special case. The paper's benchmark draws 1,000 pairs of subcatchments with mean transit times chosen at random from a log-uniform distribution between 0.1 and 20 years (pairs differing by less than a factor of two excluded), mixes each pair one to one, and asks two questions of the mixed cycle: what mean transit time does eq. 10 infer, and how well does the amplitude ratio predict the average young water fraction of the pair? Then it does the same with eight tributaries built by merging two-tributary catchments, so that the tributaries themselves are no longer gamma-distributed. Press the button; the page generates its own thousand catchments and plots the paper's Figs. 7, 11 and 13.

What to expect, from the paper: for two tributaries, root-mean-square errors of 0.009 (α = 0.5 and 1) and 0.021 (random α) in Fyw; with eight tributaries 0.009, 0.013 and 0.027, with "a slight underestimation bias" that adding phase information largely removes (Fig. 14). Mean transit times, by contrast, "can underestimate true whole-catchment MTTs by an order of magnitude or more", reliably only "when both subcatchments have MTTs of much less than 1 year" — in heterogeneous systems, "roughly 0.2 years or so — in other words, only when most of the streamflow is young water". Your run will differ in detail (different random draws) and agree in kind.
15 · How much does the shape matter?

A few percent versus a factor of a hundred

The one thing Fyw cannot escape is that its threshold age depends on α, which is usually unknown. The paper's sensitivity test (Section 4.2 and Fig. 12) measures the damage. Suppose you have measured AS/AP = 0.2. If α = 1, the mean transit time is 0.78 years (the paper prints 0.80) and 21.5 % of flow is younger than 2.27 months; if α = 0.5, the mean transit time is 1.99 years — a factor of 2.5 — while the fraction younger than the same 2.27 months is 24.2 %, a shift of 3 percentage points. Over the full order-of-magnitude range of α, the young water fraction varies by less than a factor of two and the mean transit time by more than a hundred. 16 · Three vocabularies, one question

Event water, young water, mean transit time

The four readings use three different words for "how recent is this water", and they are not interchangeable. Event water is defined by a storm: the rain of this event against everything stored before it, resolved in hours. Young water is defined by a seasonal cycle: the share of flow younger than about two to three months, whatever its path. Mean transit time is a property of the whole distribution, dominated by its long tail — the part seasonal cycles cannot see. 18 · Reading the four sources

Four readings, three blocks, one equation

Klaus and McDonnell is the week's anchor: a review that is also an argument, about a method that changed hydrology and then stopped changing it. The two Evaristo papers are paired in one block because they are the same calculation pointed at two different questions — one at a swamp and the aquifer under it, with a hydraulic witness alongside; one at trees, with a Bayesian alternative alongside. Kirchner is the week's theory paper, and the one most likely to change how you read every mean transit time you meet afterwards. The guides below are maps, not substitutes: what each asks, where its load-bearing figures live, which equations to recognise, which numbers to be able to quote, and where a careful reader might press. The notes and the critique are yours to write.

Block 1 · Core reading · the week's anchor

Klaus, J., & McDonnell, J. J. (2013). Hydrograph separation using stable isotopes: Review and evaluation. Journal of Hydrology, 505, 47–64.

Fifty years of hydrograph separation and forty of isotope hydrograph separation; the five assumptions and what twenty years of field work did to them; multi-component separations and the tracers that go with isotopes (Table 1); the method's travels outside humid forests (Table 2); end-member characterisation, uncertainty, and five controls on the partitioning; a way forward in dual isotopes and high-frequency sampling. doi:10.1016/j.jhydrol.2013.09.006

Block 2a · Paired reading

Evaristo, J., Jackson, C. R., & Rasmussen, T. C. (2025). Not so isolated: isotopic and hydraulic evidence of vertical connectivity between the Okefenokee Swamp and Floridan Aquifer. Environmental Research: Water, 1, 045005.

Two-end-member mixing of δ¹⁸O (and δ²H) between swamp water and regional Floridan water, with Genereux uncertainty; line-conditioned excess; a coastward δ¹⁸O gradient twice the 1997 one; 1,135 days of swamp and aquifer levels, their correlations, and a deconvolved step response fitted with a vertical-diffusivity type curve (D ≈ 291 m² d⁻¹); a water-budget residual of 130–220 mm yr⁻¹. Open access. doi:10.1088/3033-4942/ae2653

Block 2b · Paired reading (supplementary list)

Evaristo, J., McDonnell, J. J., & Clemens, J. (2017). Plant source water apportionment using stable isotopes: A comparison of simple linear, two-compartment mixing model approaches. Hydrological Processes, 31(21), 3750–3758.

Thirty-five tree species, bulk soil water and bore groundwater at two Christchurch plots, one irrigated; a two-compartment mass balance solved with δ²H and with δ¹⁸O; the magnitude of hydrogen isotopic separation as a test for uptake fractionation; a Bayesian mixing model (SIAR) with both isotopes; Genereux uncertainty against a Bayesian credible interval. doi:10.1002/hyp.11233

Block 3 · Supplementary reading · open access

Kirchner, J. W. (2016). Aggregation in environmental systems – Part 1: Seasonal tracer cycles quantify young water fractions, but not mean transit times, in spatially heterogeneous catchments. Hydrology and Earth System Sciences, 20, 279–297.

Sine-wave fitting of seasonal tracer cycles under gamma transit-time distributions (eqs. 1–11); a two-subcatchment thought experiment (Figs. 4–6) and 1,000-catchment benchmark tests (Figs. 7–8, 11, 13–14) showing that inferred mean transit times are biased low by large factors while amplitude ratios aggregate linearly; the young water fraction (eqs. 13–14, Figs. 9–10) as an aggregation-proof alternative; sensitivity to the shape factor (Fig. 12); implications for the MTT literature. doi:10.5194/hess-20-279-2016

Cross-paper synthesis, in one line: every one of these papers is a mixing calculation, and every one of them is rescued or undone by the same two questions — are these really the end-members, and does the answer survive its own error bars? The review finds the field still arguing about end-members forty years on; the swamp paper gets two answers from two isotopes and adds a hydraulic witness; the tree paper shows a hidden fractionation turning one isotope's answer upside down; and Kirchner shows that even with perfect data, the quantity you ask for decides whether heterogeneity will wreck the answer. The question that carries forward: for your proposal's tracer, what are the end-members, how would you know if one were missing, and which quantity — a fraction or an age — will survive the catchment's heterogeneity?
