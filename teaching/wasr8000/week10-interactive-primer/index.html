Old Helium, Rare Oxygen, Chosen Water

An interactive primer on three ways of reading water that the course calls advanced and emerging. A clock the rock winds from below: radiogenic helium-4 in the Great Artesian Basin, accumulating seventy-four times faster than the aquifer can make it. A third oxygen isotope whose entire signal is a difference of 0.011 between two slopes: triple oxygen isotopes and Δ′¹⁷O, measured in parts per million. And a Sierra Nevada creek read with three cosmogenic clocks and oxygen-18 at once, which turns out to release its oldest water in a drought and only mildly favour its youngest in a storm. Builds on Weeks 2, 7 and 9; no tracer background needed.

1 · The opening question

Seventy-four times too much helium

In the Jurassic sandstone of the Great Artesian Basin, Torgersen and Clarke sampled helium from wells along two flow lines stretching 600 km across central Queensland. Near the recharge area, where radiocarbon still works (ages of 8.5 to 36.6 thousand years), the helium-4 in the water rises with age at 4.6 × 10⁻¹² cm³ STP per cm³ of water per year — almost exactly what the uranium (1.7 ppm) and thorium (6.1 ppm) in the sandstone can supply by alpha decay, 3.95 × 10⁻¹². Further out, where ages come from Darcy's law and run from 0.15 to 0.60 million years, the helium climbs at 2.91 × 10⁻¹⁰: seventy-four times the in-situ supply. The rock is uniform; its ²²²Rn says so, varying by only ±50 % about its mean along the whole 600 km. Which explanation would you defend?

This is the week's first argument in miniature: a clock is only as good as the rate you assume it runs at, and the rate may not belong to the rock the water sits in. Sections 2–6 follow the helium case step by step; Sections 8–13 turn to a tracer whose whole signal is a two-per-cent difference between two slopes; Sections 14–19 take three decay clocks and oxygen-18 to a creek that chooses which water to let go.

2 · The rock's slow breath

A clock that counts alpha particles

Every atom of uranium-238 that decays to lead sheds eight alpha particles along the way; uranium-235 sheds seven and thorium-232 six. Each alpha particle is a helium-4 nucleus. It stops within 30–100 μm of where it was born, grabs two electrons, and is helium gas — stable, inert, and with nowhere to go but the pore water, if it was born close enough to a grain surface to reach it. Because U and Th have half-lives of billions of years their supply is constant on any hydrologic timescale, so the helium concentration of a parcel of water should grow in a straight line with its age. That is the ⁴He clock as proposed in 1936 and reviewed by Davis and Bentley in 1982: measure the helium, divide by the rate. The trouble, as the introduction to the paper records, is that ⁴He ages "tend to be greater, oftentimes by factors of up to 60 times" than ¹⁴C or hydrologic ages. The rate, not the measurement, is the weak link. Click a card to meet the four quantities the paper works with, then try the rate equation yourself.

The in-situ supply rate (eq. 1)

Torgersen's 1980 relation gives the ⁴He production per gram of rock from the uranium and thorium contents (both in ppm; expanded, U* = 0.508 [U] + 0.123 [Th], so the thorium chain counts for about a quarter as much helium per ppm as uranium's), and converting it to a rate per cubic centimetre of pore water needs the rock density, the porosity, and the fraction ΛHe of the helium that actually escapes the grains. The paper takes ΛHe = 1: with grains of 100 μm and an alpha recoil range of 30–100 μm at least 23 % and probably nearly all of the helium is "immediately available", and over a million years the rest diffuses out of the grains anyway. 3 · The basin

Two slopes in one aquifer

Table 1 of the paper lists 39 analyses from 22 wells (helium for 35 of them, many in duplicate) with ²²²Rn, ²²⁶Ra, temperature, distance from the recharge area, and two kinds of age: ¹⁴C ages from Calf and Habermehl for the recharge-area wells, and hydrologic ages from Bentley et al. for the flow lines. Helium was taken in welded stainless-steel cylinders through tubing inserted into the well-head, flushed to exclude air; the concentrations are "good to ±5 %", but duplicates from hot artesian wells sometimes disagree because the water degasses as it rises — at the steam-emitting Innamincka-1 the two analyses differ by a factor of three. Reading an age off the clock

A ⁴He age is the excess helium divided by an accumulation rate: t = (C − C₀)/rate, with C₀ the air-saturated baseline. Pick a well and compare the age the clock gives under the in-situ rate with the age it gives under the main-basin rate — and with the hydrologic age the paper used to calibrate it.

Two things to notice. The main-basin rate is a line fitted to the hydrologic ages, so it cannot test those ages; it is calibrated by them. And the fit leans on two wells: this page's refit of the 25 main-basin analyses returns the paper's 2.91 × 10⁻¹⁰ exactly, with the line crossing zero at about 0.2 Myr, but dropping the two Innamincka-1 analyses (34,000 and 13,000 — the pair that disagree by a factor of three) halves the slope to 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁰. That is still 36 times the in-situ supply, so the conclusion survives; the number does not. A discussant might ask what the ±0.54 quoted on the rate was computed from.
4 · Three suspects

Eliminating the easy explanations

The paper lists three possible sources for the extra helium — in-situ steady-state production, release of helium trapped in the mineral grains by weathering, and sources outside the confined bed — and spends its middle pages disposing of the first two with arithmetic. Click a suspect for the case against it; then use the diffusion calculator to see why the grains of a Jurassic sandstone hold almost no helium to release.

How much helium can a grain keep? (eqs. 2–3)

Mussett's expression for the fraction of a gas lost from a sphere by diffusion over geologic time, and its complement, the fraction retained. The paper takes the diffusivity of helium in basaltic glass — 10⁻¹⁷ to 4 × 10⁻¹⁴ cm² s⁻¹ — as an upper limit for the sandstone, and finds that even the slow end of that range (10⁻¹⁷) leaves essentially nothing retained after 200 million years; only the "unlikely low" value of 10⁻²⁸ — the diffusivity of argon in feldspar at 100 °C — keeps 61 %. The weathering release is then that retained helium times the rate at which feldspar dissolves (about 10⁻¹³ mol per cm³ of water per year, or 3 × 10⁻¹¹ g of rock).

5 · The whole crust as the source

Forty-four kilometres of sandstone, or one crust

If the helium source has the sandstone's own U and Th content, how thick would it have to be to supply 74 times the aquifer's production? Six hundred metres times 74 — 44 km, roughly the thickness of the continental crust under the basin. So the paper asks the question the other way round: what does the whole crust produce? Taylor and McLennan give the upper crust 2.8 ppm U with Th/U = 3.8 and the lower crust 0.28 ppm; seismic work puts the upper–lower boundary at 20–24 km and the Moho at about 40 km. Eq. 1 per gram, times density, times thickness, gives a flux per square centimetre of surface. Divide that flux by the aquifer's thickness and porosity, and you have the rate at which helium would accumulate in its water if the aquifer trapped all of it. Two basins, two thicknesses, one flux: that is the argument.
6 · Can the helium escape?

Why the trap holds, and why the clock starts late

A flux from below only accumulates if the aquifer does not leak it out through the top. The paper bounds the loss with Darcy's law: the vertical water velocity through the confining mudstones and shales, times a maximum helium concentration taken as 1.3 × 10⁻⁴ cm³ STP cm⁻³ (The Gap's value in Table 1; Innamincka-1's 3.4 × 10⁻⁴ is higher), is the most helium that can leave with the leaking water. With a "fast" shale conductivity of 10⁻⁷ cm s⁻¹, a pressure gradient of 0.05 m m⁻¹ and a shale porosity below 0.05, that loss comes to less than a third of the crustal flux; with an average shale (10⁻⁹ cm s⁻¹, porosity 0.01) it is, in the paper's words, "even less significant". And the proof that the loss is small is in the data: if the flux out ever matched the flux in, the helium could no longer rise with age — "the observation that ⁴He concentration continues to increase implies that the loss terms are small compared to the source terms". Try the leakage sum, then the second subtlety: the delay before a flux entering at the base of a 600 m aquifer reaches wells that, for economic reasons, are screened in its top 20 %.

Two consequences the data show. In the recharge area the clock runs at the in-situ rate — the crustal flux has not yet diffused up to the sampled depth — and in the main basin it runs at the crustal rate: the paper's conclusions (1) and (2). And the fitted main-basin line does not pass through the origin: on Fig. 2 it crosses zero helium at roughly 0.2 Myr (about 0.23 Myr by this page's reading of the figure, 0.21 by its own refit — a reading, not a number the paper states), which is just what a delayed crossover would produce. The paper's last calculation turns the question on the crust itself: even with an "unlikely (unreasonable)" diffusivity of 10⁻⁵ cm² s⁻¹, the response time of a 40 km crust would be 5 × 10¹⁰ years, far longer than the age of the Earth. The authors list four ways out, as possibilities rather than requirements: U and Th concentrated near the surface (Jaupart et al.'s e-folding depths of 7–15 km for U), a partial approach to a diffusive steady state in the near-surface crust, a deep-crust gradient established before the Jurassic, and "facilitated transport" at 10⁻⁶–10⁻⁵ cm² s⁻¹ by the fluids that metamorphism is thought to require. Whole-crust degassing, they conclude, "probably requires" rock fracturing and "may require" facilitated transport — a mechanism argued in a few sentences here and, as Week 7 flagged, "far from being universally accepted".
7 · What counts as emerging

Old ideas with new instruments

None of this week's tracers is new in principle. The helium clock was proposed in 1936; ¹⁷O was dismissed for decades as "invariant, too difficult to measure, or redundant"; sodium-22 and sulfur-35 have rained out of the sky for as long as cosmic rays have hit argon. What has changed is what can be measured: a Δ′¹⁷O to ±10 per meg, a ³⁵S activity of a few millibecquerels per litre, a ²²Na count in a long precipitation collection. The course plan's third learning goal is to situate these against the established methods. Click a card for what each reads, what makes it attractive, and what is still unsettled; the metal stable isotopes are here as labelled background from the further-reading list, not from a block reading.

A working definition for the week: a tracer is "emerging" when its measurement has outrun its interpretation — when laboratories can produce the number more reliably than the field can say what it means. Each of this week's three papers is, in that sense, an attempt to close the gap from the interpretation side: a mechanism for helium, a process framework for Δ′¹⁷O, a storage-selection model for the cosmogenic trio.
8 · The third oxygen

Three isotopes, and a notation that keeps them straight

Oxygen has three stable isotopes: ¹⁶O at 99.757 %, ¹⁸O at 0.205 %, and ¹⁷O, the rarest, at 0.038 %. Week 2 taught you to track ¹⁸O/¹⁶O as δ¹⁸O, and to read d-excess as a departure from the meteoric water line. The third isotope would be redundant if every process shifted ¹⁷O/¹⁶O by exactly a fixed fraction of the ¹⁸O/¹⁶O shift. Mass-dependent fractionation nearly does that — the ratios are related by a power law, α¹⁷ = (α¹⁸)θ — but the exponent θ is not quite the same for every process — 0.529 for liquid–vapour equilibrium, 0.518 for diffusion through air — and that "not quite", a difference of 0.011 or about two per cent of the slope, is the entire signal. To see it you must first stop using δ. Over a wide enough range a power law is a curve in δ–δ space, and the curvature alone would produce an apparent "excess" of tens of per meg that has nothing to do with hydrology. The logarithmic δ′ = ln(δ + 1) makes the power law exactly straight, and Δ′¹⁷O is then defined as the vertical distance from a reference line of slope 0.528. Per meg, and why three decimals. Δ′¹⁷O is quoted in per meg — parts per million, a thousand per meg to the per mil — because natural meteoric values mostly sit between about −10 and +40 per meg and the best laboratories resolve about ±10. The review's first analytical rule follows: report δ¹⁸O and δ¹⁷O to three decimal places, or the Δ′¹⁷O computed from them is lost in rounding. Its second: the term. ∆′¹⁷O, ∆¹⁷O, ¹⁷O-excess and ¹⁷Oexcess all appear in the literature and all mean eq. 5; the review recommends Δ′¹⁷O because the prime says the δ′ values were used and the capital delta says it is a deviation from a reference, the convention shared by Δ³⁴S and Δ²⁵Mg.
9 · Two exponents

A signal that lives in a two-per-cent difference of slope

Three numbers carry the whole subject. The equilibrium exponent for liquid–vapour exchange is θeq = 0.529 — predicted by mass-law theory and confirmed to ±0.001 by Barkan and Luz in 2005 — and it does not change with temperature. The kinetic exponent for water vapour diffusing through air is θdiff = 0.518 (0.5185 ± 0.0003 measured; 0.5184 from the kinetic theory of gases). And the reference slope λref = 0.528 — the slope meteoric waters happened to fit (0.5281 ± 0.0015 for Meijer and Li in 1998; 0.528 ± 0.0001 for Luz and Barkan in 2010), adopted by convention and, the review admits, "somewhat arbitrary" — sits between them, much closer to θeq. The rule that follows is simple enough to carry in your head: a process with exponent θ changes Δ′¹⁷O by (θ − λref) times its change in δ′¹⁸O. Equilibrium processes move a water almost parallel to the reference line and change Δ′¹⁷O by about one per meg per per-mil; diffusion moves it ten times more steeply, ten per meg per per-mil. That ten-fold contrast — not the size of any one effect — is what lets a single extra isotope separate kinetic from equilibrium fractionation. Pick a process, or set your own exponent.

The vocabulary of slopes (Section 5.2 of the review). θ is the exponent of a single fractionating process. λ is the slope of a line through data that integrate several processes: λobs for any particular data set, λmwl for a meteoric water line, λref for the fixed reference frame of eq. 5. And λRayleigh = (¹⁷α − 1)/(¹⁸α − 1), the slope of rain-out, is 0.5278 at 25 °C and 0.5272 at −25 °C (Table 5) — so close to λref that Rayleigh distillation, the process that governs almost everything about δ¹⁸O, barely moves Δ′¹⁷O at all. That is one reason the review gives for keeping 0.528 as the reference: it "removes most Δ′¹⁷O effects from Rayleigh distillation", so that what remains visible is humidity.
10 · From ocean to rain

Why meteoric water has positive Δ′¹⁷O

The review's Figs. 9–11 walk the Craig–Gordon model of evaporation step by step, and the steps explain the sign of almost every meteoric water on Earth. Sea water evaporates into a saturated layer in equilibrium with it: the vapour is lighter by ln αeq — about 9.3 ‰ in δ′¹⁸O at 25 °C — along the slope θeq = 0.529, so its Δ′¹⁷O is slightly negative, −9 per meg (−11 at 5 °C). The vapour then diffuses through the unsaturated air above, and diffusion fractionates along θdiff = 0.518: a few more per mil lighter, but now Δ′¹⁷O swings strongly positive, the more so the drier the air. Condensation back to liquid follows θeq again and adds another ten per meg or so to the rain. Rain-out along the way (Rayleigh distillation) changes δ′¹⁸O by many per mil and Δ′¹⁷O by almost nothing. 11 · What pushes Δ′¹⁷O down

Evaporation, and the arithmetic of mixing

Two processes make negative Δ′¹⁷O, and only one of them is physical. Evaporation after condensation — from a lake, a soil, a leaf — runs the diffusion step in reverse: the escaping vapour takes the high Δ′¹⁷O, and what remains grows heavier in δ¹⁸O and lower in Δ′¹⁷O along a slope near θdiff, "potentially > 200 per meg in plant water, typically no more than ~50–60 per meg in surface water" (Table 7). The review gives the equations for a lake at hydrologic steady state (eq. 10) and for a pan evaporating to dryness (eqs. 11–13); the left panel integrates the pan case. The other process is mixing. Two waters with the same Δ′¹⁷O but different δ¹⁸O mix to a water with lower Δ′¹⁷O, purely because δ′ is a logarithm and logarithms do not mix linearly: half VSMOW and half SLAP, both at zero, gives −100 per meg (Fig. 13). 12 · One line or many?

The meteoric water line that would not hold still

Luz and Barkan's 2010 global line, δ′¹⁷O = 0.528 δ′¹⁸O + 0.033, was built from two standards, 29 Antarctic snow samples and 52 waters mostly from Europe and Asia, and it set the expectation that Δ′¹⁷O of meteoric water stays near 33 per meg across 70 ‰ of δ′¹⁸O. A decade of data — and the review's own 104 new rivers and lakes from six continents and 17 Köppen classes — says otherwise. Rivers and monthly precipitation from −20.5 to +9.4 ‰ in δ¹⁸O fit a line with slope 0.5267 and intercept 0.013 ‰ (eq. 7; 0.5268 and 0.015 without the arid-region rivers, eq. 8). Waters below −20 ‰ fit slope 0.5285 and intercept 0.0461 ‰ (eq. 9) — a line the review had to build from every sample in that range, more than 97 % of them polar snow and ice, because only 12 samples there meet the precipitation-or-river criterion that Craig's and Dansgaard's lines were built on. 13 · Measuring per meg

Two ways to count a rare isotope, and the rules that make them comparable

Ten per meg is one part in a hundred thousand. Two kinds of instrument reach it. The classical route fluorinates the water with cobalt(III) fluoride — 2 H₂O + 4 CoF₃ → O₂ + 4 HF + 4 CoF₂ (eq. 16) — and runs the oxygen on a dual-inlet mass spectrometer at masses 32, 33 and 34; the review's own laboratory injects ~2 μL into a 360–370 °C nickel reactor, purifies the O₂ through traps and molecular sieve for just over an hour, and then integrates 40 cycles of 50 seconds each. The laser route vaporises the water into a cavity ring-down or cavity-enhanced absorption cell and reads all three ratios at once, in a smaller room, but needs integration times of up to 20 minutes per injection or more than twenty repeats to reach the same precision. Compare them, then test whether your own samples could ever show a Δ′¹⁷O signal.

Would Δ′¹⁷O resolve anything here?

The review's rule of thumb (Section 7.2.7): under non-steady-state evaporation the slope of Δ′¹⁷O against d-excess is at most about 2 per meg per ‰ (0.7–2 in published data), so a sample set needs more than about 5 ‰ of d-excess range before its Δ′¹⁷O range can exceed a ±10 per meg precision. The normalization rules

  • VSMOW–SLAP at both ends of every session, ideally within it too, because instrument non-linearities drift (Section 7.2.1). Define δ¹⁷O of SLAP from the defined δ¹⁸O (−55.5 ‰), Δ′¹⁷O = 0 and λref — the same two-point stretch most laboratories already apply to δ¹⁸O.
  • Secondary reference waters (GISP, USGS 45–50) as 5–10 % of every session, spread through it, to catch drift and to prove accuracy; their Δ′¹⁷O values are still laboratory-reported, not certified (Table 9).
  • Bracket the unknowns. Reference waters span natural δ¹⁸O but not natural Δ′¹⁷O; the review suggests evaporated snow or furnace condensate as in-house standards with Δ′¹⁷O below −350 per meg, inherited from atmospheric O₂ (about −400 to −500).
  • Run in order of δ¹⁸O with preparatory injections to clear memory: one when consecutive samples are within 5 ‰, two when they differ by more.
  • Report precision and accuracy, the drift and memory corrections, the standards, the replicate counts, and the λref used — and the data to three decimals.

A small press-on point: the review prints δ¹⁷OSLAP = −29.6968 ‰; the arithmetic above gives −29.6986 ‰ — the two differ in their last two digits, by 2 per meg, whichever is the slip. Below the quoted precision, but the kind of thing a reader who recomputes will notice.

Why precision in Δ′¹⁷O is better than in either δ. Sample handling fractionates water vapour, but it does so mass-dependently, so the errors in δ¹⁸O and δ¹⁷O move together along a slope close to 0.528 and nearly cancel in eq. 5. The review's laboratory reports root-mean-square errors on USGS waters of 0.9 ‰ in δ¹⁸O and 0.3 ‰ in δ¹⁷O — poor by Picarro standards — and 10 per meg in Δ′¹⁷O, which is what matters. Precision in the difference does not require precision in the parts.
14 · A Sierra creek with four clocks

Providence Creek, and what fell on it

The Southern Sierra Critical Zone Observatory sits at 1,800–2,100 m on a tread of the stepped Sierra Nevada, 235 km inland from the Pacific, at the rain–snow transition: a subalpine Mediterranean climate, 8 °C mean annual temperature, 1,050 ± 535 mm of precipitation of which more than 80 % falls in autumn and winter. Visser and colleagues sampled the 1 km² P301 subcatchment of Providence Creek (4 km²; steep, deeply weathered granodiorite, three eroding meadows) nearly monthly through water years 2015 and 2016 — the last, extreme year of the 2012–2015 drought and a near-average year that followed it — and measured four isotopes in the same water: tritium (half-life 12.32 yr), sodium-22 (2.6 yr), sulfur-35 (87 days) and oxygen-18 (stable). Three decay clocks with windows of months, a few years and decades, and one seasonal label. The tiles give the water balance; the cards give the inputs; the chart below is their Table 1.

15 · Tritium rises with flow

A correlation that rules out a fixed age distribution

At low flow (below 0.05 mm d⁻¹) the stream's tritium was 6.4–8.3 pCi L⁻¹, averaging 7.6 — 56 % of the 13.5 that falls as precipitation. During the March 2016 storm, the one high-flow sample, it reached 10.9, 81 % of the input. Across all 25 samples tritium is linearly correlated with the square root of stream flow (R² = 0.75, p < 0.001); sulfur-35 is too, more weakly (R² = 0.37, p < 0.01). Read naively as a single piston-flow decay age, 56 % is ten years and 81 % is four; read as the mean of an exponential age distribution, 14 years and 4. The point is not the numbers but their motion: the age distribution of the stream changes with the state of the catchment, which no time-invariant transit-time model can produce. 16 · Three windows

Why one clock cannot tell you the shape

Each decay clock sees a different part of the age distribution. Sulfur-35 is all but gone within a year or so (five half-lives is 1.2 years), so it counts only the youngest water; sodium-22 fades over a decade; tritium over several. Under an exponential age distribution the three should agree on one mean age — and at Providence Creek they do not. Tritium at 56 % of its input and sodium-22 at about 20 % of its input both imply a mean age of about 14–15 years under an exponential model; sulfur-35 at the text's 28 % implies under a year, and even at Table 1's 13 % only about two years. The only way to satisfy all three is a distribution with a thin slice of very young water on top of a much older bulk. 17 · How a catchment chooses

StorAge Selection with a beta function

Rank all the water in the catchment by age, youngest first, and let PS(τ < T) be the fraction of storage younger than T. A storage-selection function says which part of that ranking today's discharge is drawn from: PQ = Ix(PS; a, b), a cumulative beta function of the storage rank. With a = b = 1 the stream is a fair sample of storage; a < 1 favours the young end; b < 1 favours the old end. The paper's trick is to let the preference depend on the state of the catchment through an index i that runs linearly with √Q between the second and 98th percentiles of flow (eqs. 5–7): in the wettest conditions a drops to aQmin with b = 1, in the driest b drops to bQmin with a = 1, and the hand-over happens at a single flow (√Q ≈ 2 for the best-fit parameters): below it — most days — the stream prefers old water, above it young. Evapotranspiration gets a constant preference for young water, aET. Four parameters, with the long-term active storage SA, and 18,000 combinations tested. Set them.

19 · Three metres of water

How the level of δ¹⁸O weighed a catchment

The tracers left two parameters locked together: a larger storage SA could always be compensated by a weaker young-water preference in evapotranspiration (larger aET), because taking older water out through the plants removes "accumulated age" from storage and leaves the stream's tritium unchanged. What broke the tie was a number tritium cannot see: the stream's δ¹⁸O sat 0.6–1.5 ‰ below the precipitation-weighted input, winter-biased, all year. If evapotranspiration draws the youngest water — which in the season of highest ET is the summer rain, the heaviest water the catchment receives — then what is left for the stream is biased toward winter. The stronger the preference, the lighter the stream. The level, not the seasonal swing, fixed aET = 0.25, and with it SA = 3 m. The storage, three ways. The fitted active storage is 3 m of water averaged over the catchment, varying from 2.7 m in the drought to nearly 4.0 m in 2011. Three methods that agree are not three proofs — "neither estimate is precise" — but the hydrochronological one is the only one that weighs the whole landscape. One more sum the paper does not do: 3 m of storage turning over 1.05 m yr⁻¹ of water has a turnover time of 2.9 years, yet the mean age of storage in Table 3 is 4.2 years. A well-mixed reservoir would give the same number for both; the difference is the plants taking the young water first.
